Heuristics, Greedy search & A*

By the end of this page you can invent a heuristic for a problem you have never seen, hand-trace Greedy and A* to the last decimal, say exactly when A* is optimal and when it silently is not — and prove the blocking argument that the slides leave as “TODO”.

Where we are

Chapter 1 built the machinery: state spaces, the generic frontier loop, BFS, DFS and uniform-cost search. UCS is complete and optimal, and it has exactly one flaw: it has never heard of the goal. It spends its whole life expanding cheap nodes in every direction, including directly away from the thing you asked it to find. This chapter gives search a nose. Everything below assumes you already know what a frontier, a node, $g(n)$ and the explored set are; if any of those are shaky, read Chapter 1 first — this page will not re-teach them.

1. What UCS is missing

Here is the professor's own framing, and it is worth taking literally: Donuts ASAP! You are standing somewhere in a city and you want a donut from a specific shop as fast as possible. Uniform-cost search's plan for getting there is to walk one block in every direction, then two blocks in every direction, then three, until one of those expanding rings happens to contain the donut shop.

That is not stupid. It is provably correct — the first goal UCS pops is the cheapest one, which is the entire point of Chapter 1's optimality proof. But it is wasteful in a very specific and fixable way.

Intuition

UCS's cost contours are circles around the start. If the goal is 3 km east, UCS still explores 3 km west, 3 km north and 3 km south before it gets there — because nothing in the algorithm's inputs distinguishes east from west. The information “the goal is east” exists in your head and in the map, but UCS's interface to the world is only $\text{Actions}$, $\text{Result}$, $c$ and $\text{IsGoal}$, and none of those four functions ever mentions where the goal is. We are going to hand the algorithm a fifth function whose entire job is to whisper “warmer / colder”.

2. What a heuristic is

Definition — heuristic

A heuristic is a function $$h : \text{States} \to \R_{\ge 0}$$ that estimates how close a state is to a goal. It is designed for a particular search problem — there is no universal heuristic, the same way there is no universal state space.

Read the notation carefully: $h(n)$ is written as a function of a node $n$ but it only ever looks at that node's state. It does not know how you got there, it does not look at $g(n)$, and it does not get to see the future. It is a cheap guess computed from the state alone.

The two examples the course gives, both for pathing problems:

  • Manhattan distance between $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$: $|x_1-x_2| + |y_1-y_2|$. The distance if you may only move along grid lines — the right estimate for a Pacman maze, where diagonal moves do not exist.
  • Euclidean distance: $\sqrt{(x_1-x_2)^2 + (y_1-y_2)^2}$. The as-the-crow-flies distance — the right estimate when you can move in any direction, or when you are on a road map and want the straight-line distance between two towns.

The slide's little triangle makes the point in one picture: a state that is 10 units across and 5 units up from the goal is $10+5=15$ away in Manhattan terms and $\sqrt{100+25} \approx 11.2$ away in Euclidean terms. Both are estimates of the same thing; they differ because they model different movement rules.

Analogy

A heuristic is the altitude reading on a hiker's watch when the goal is the valley floor. It doesn't know the trail. It doesn't know about the cliff between you and the valley. But it's cheap to read, and “I'm at 800 m” versus “I'm at 200 m” is real information about which way is probably home.

Where the analogy breaks: the watch can be badly wrong in a way that matters — if the only trail down goes up first, altitude will send you into a cliff. That failure mode is exactly Greedy search, and the fix (A*) is to keep track of how far you have already walked as well.

The running example: straight-line distance to Bucharest

The course uses the Romania road map from Russell & Norvig. The search problem: drive from Arad to Bucharest. States are cities; actions are “take road $X$”; step costs are road lengths in kilometres; the goal test is “am I in Bucharest?”.

The heuristic $h_{\text{SLD}}(n)$ is the straight-line distance from city $n$ to Bucharest — the number you would get by laying a ruler on the map, ignoring that roads exist. It is computable instantly from latitude and longitude, it requires no search, and it is never an overestimate, because no road can be shorter than the straight line. Hold on to that last sentence; in §7 it becomes the definition of admissible.

The two kinds of number live in completely different worlds — one is measured, one is imagined — and the single best idea on this page is that you should add them together anyway.

The effect of a heuristic

The slide's picture of “effect of heuristics” is two blobs, and it says: guide search towards the goal instead of all over the place. Concretely, on the Romania map, an uninformed search sitting in Arad has no reason to prefer Sibiu ($h=253$) over Timisoara ($h=329$) or Zerind ($h=374$) — all three are just “neighbours”, and UCS orders them purely by road length, which actually makes Zerind (75 km) look best. It is the only direction that is unambiguously wrong.

With a heuristic, Sibiu's $253$ against Zerind's $374$ says “this way is 120 km more promising”, and the search leans east before it has spent a single kilometre finding out. That lean is the whole benefit. The rest of this page is about how far you are allowed to lean before you lean over.

Common mistake

“$h$ is the distance to the goal.” No — $h$ is an estimate of the distance to the goal, and the entire theory below exists because it can be wrong. The true distance has its own name, $h^*(n)$, and if you could compute $h^*$ cheaply you would not need to search at all: you would just walk downhill on $h^*$ and arrive. (A* with $h = h^*$ expands only the nodes on an optimal path. See §15.)

3. Greedy best-first search

The most obvious way to use a heuristic is also the most reckless one.

Definition — Greedy (best-first) search

Greedy search is the generic search algorithm with a priority-queue frontier ordered by $h(n)$ alone. At every step it expands the node that looks closest to the goal, and it never once consults how much that node already cost to reach.

In other words: take UCS's pseudocode and replace the priority $g(n)$ with $h(n)$. That is the entire algorithm.

Analogy

Greedy is the friend who navigates by pointing at the destination and walking that way. In an open field they are unbeatable and you will feel foolish for having opened a map. In a city with a river through it, they will march you to the riverbank, and then along the riverbank, and then — after you have burned an hour — back the way you came to the bridge you passed in minute two.

Case 1 — where Greedy wins big

Take the Romania problem, Arad → Bucharest. Greedy expands Arad, looks at $\{$Zerind 374, Sibiu 253, Timisoara 329$\}$, and pops Sibiu. From Sibiu it sees $\{$Arad 366, Oradea 380, Fagaras 176, Rimnicu Vilcea 193$\}$ and pops Fagaras. From Fagaras it sees Bucharest, $h=0$, pops it — goal.

Three expansions. Zero wasted nodes. UCS on the same problem expands Zerind, Timisoara, Sibiu, Rimnicu Vilcea, Fagaras, Pitesti, Craiova and more before it dares return an answer. For the price of one ruler measurement per city, Greedy found a perfectly usable 450 km route almost instantly. When you need a route rather than the route, this is a genuinely excellent trade.

Case 2 — where Greedy returns a worse path

That same 450 km route is not optimal. The optimal route is 418 km, through Rimnicu Vilcea and Pitesti. Watch exactly where Greedy lost it, because the mechanism is always the same:

Greedy cannot see that, because $h$ is an estimate of a quantity it never checks, and because Greedy never compares total plans — only next steps. The estimate was not even a bad one (176 is a fine estimate of 310's straight-line component); it simply ranked two options in the wrong order, which is all it takes.

Case 3 — where Greedy thrashes, or never terminates

Now put Greedy in Iasi and ask for Fagaras. Iasi's neighbours are Neamt and Vaslui. Neamt is geometrically closer to Fagaras, so Greedy drives to Neamt — which is a dead end whose only road goes back to Iasi.

  • In graph search (with an explored set) Greedy now backs out, expands Vaslui, and eventually gets there — having wasted the entire detour.
  • In tree search (no explored set) Greedy goes Iasi → Neamt → Iasi → Neamt → … forever, because from Neamt the best-looking neighbour is Iasi and from Iasi the best-looking neighbour is Neamt. The search never terminates on a finite graph. That is the technical meaning of “Greedy is not complete”.
Verdict — Greedy search
  • Complete? No as tree search — it can loop between two states forever (Iasi ↔ Neamt). Yes as graph search on a finite state space — the explored set stops repeats, so it must eventually run out of new states to expand.
  • Optimal? No, in either form, for any interesting $h$. It chooses by $h$ alone, so it has literally no representation of path cost; there is nothing in the algorithm that could prefer a cheaper plan. Arad → Bucharest returns 450 when 418 exists.
  • Worst case is the same as DFS — $O(b^m)$ time and space, where $b$ is the branching factor and $m$ the maximum depth — because a bad heuristic can send it down one wrong branch forever.
  • Best case is spectacular: with a perfect heuristic it walks straight to the goal in $d$ expansions.
Greedy search with $h(n) = 0$ for every $n$ behaves like which algorithm?
Why: if every node has priority 0, the priority queue is entirely ties, so behaviour is decided by the tie-break: pop the oldest and you get BFS, pop the newest and you get DFS. It is not UCS, because Greedy's priority is $h$ and never $g$ — with $h \equiv 0$, Greedy throws away the only information it had. Contrast this with A* with $h \equiv 0$, whose priority is $g + 0 = g$, and which therefore is exactly UCS (§11).

4. A*: combining UCS and Greedy

Put the two failures side by side.

  • UCS orders by path cost, or backward cost $g(n)$ — the money already spent getting to $n$. It is a fact, and it is completely blind to the future.
  • Greedy orders by goal proximity, or forward cost $h(n)$ — the estimated money still to spend. It is a guess, and it is completely blind to the past.

Each one throws away exactly the half of the problem the other one keeps. So keep both halves.

Definition — A* search

A\* search is the generic search algorithm with a priority-queue frontier ordered by $$f(n) \;=\; g(n) \;+\; h(n).$$

  • $g(n)$ — the cost of the path from the start state to $n$ along the branch we actually took. Known exactly.
  • $h(n)$ — our estimate of the cheapest remaining cost from $n$'s state to a goal. A guess.
  • $f(n)$ — therefore an estimate of the total cost of the best complete solution that goes through $n$. That sentence is the whole algorithm: A* always works on the plan that currently looks cheapest end to end.
Intuition

$f$ is a price tag on a whole journey, not on a node. When A* pops the node with the smallest $f$, it is saying: “of all the half-finished plans on my desk, this one has the lowest projected total bill, so it deserves the next hour of my time.” UCS ranks half-finished plans by what they have cost so far — which favours plans that have barely started. Greedy ranks them by how close they claim to be to done — which favours plans that got there expensively. A* ranks them by projected total, which is the only ranking a person budgeting a project would ever use.

Common mistake — the tie at $d$

After Greedy pops $e$, its frontier holds two nodes for state $d$, both with $h = 2$: the good one ($g=4$) and the terrible one ($g=10$). Greedy has no way to prefer either — they tie on its only criterion — so the answer depends entirely on the tie-break, and the returned cost is 6 or 12 depending on an implementation detail that has nothing to do with the problem. That uncertainty is what “no optimality guarantee” feels like in practice. A* has no such ambiguity: $f(d_{g=4}) = 6$ and $f(d_{g=10}) = 12$ are nowhere near tied.

Takeaway: the $f$ values along the pop sequence were $7, 6, 7, 7, 8$ — they went down once, from $S$'s 7 to $A$'s 6. That dip is legal for an admissible heuristic but it is a symptom: this $h$ is admissible and not consistent (check the edge $S\!\to\!A$: $h(S) - h(A) = 7-4 = 3 > 2 = \text{cost}$). Keep that in your pocket until §10.

5. Watch all three run on the same maze

Graphs with seven nodes are good for proofs and terrible for intuition about cost. Picture the same three-way comparison on a large Pacman-style maze, with Manhattan distance as the heuristic: every free square is a state, every move costs 1, and the walls make the straight-line guess wrong in interesting places.

Key idea — the shape each one explores

Run all three and the regions they expand have three recognisable shapes. UCS's is a diamond centred on the start (a Manhattan-metric circle). A*'s expanded region is that same diamond squashed along the line to the goal. Greedy's is a narrow finger reaching straight for the goal — beautifully economical, and aimed with no guarantee whatsoever that the finger is pointing down the shortest corridor.

6. Why A* can still be wrong: a bad heuristic

A* is not magic, and the slide makes the point with a three-node graph. Here it is exactly:

Figure 4 · “Is A* optimal?” — the counterexample from the slides
The optimal plan is $S \to A \to G$ at cost $1+3 = 4$. A* returns $S \to G$ at cost 5. Nothing is wrong with A*; everything is wrong with $h(A) = 6$.

Exactly what A* does here

  1. Frontier: $\langle S,\ f = 0 + 7 = 7\rangle$. Pop $S$. Not a goal.
  2. Generate children:
    • $A$: $g = 1$, $h = 6$, so $f(A) = 1 + 6 = \mathbf{7}$.
    • $G$ (via the direct road): $g = 5$, $h = 0$, so $f(G) = 5 + 0 = \mathbf{5}$.
  3. Frontier: $\langle G,5\rangle, \langle A,7\rangle$. The minimum is $G$ at 5. Pop it. Goal test passes — A* returns $S\!-\!G$, cost 5.
  4. It never expands $A$. It never discovers that going through $A$ costs 4.

Why did the priority queue rank them that way? Because $f(A) = 7$ claims “any plan through $A$ will end up costing about 7”, and that claim is a lie: the best plan through $A$ costs 4. The lie came from $h(A) = 6$, an estimate of a true remaining cost of $h^*(A) = 3$. A* trusted it, concluded $A$ was a bad neighbourhood, and stopped looking.

Key idea — the one-line diagnosis from the slide

Estimated goal cost > actual future cost. When $h$ overestimates, A* becomes pessimistic about a region and prunes it away — and pruning without proof is exactly how you lose the optimum. So: we need estimates to be less than actual costs.

The asymmetry is worth pausing on. Underestimating is harmless to correctness: it makes A* too optimistic about a region, so it looks there, wastes time, and discovers the truth. Overestimating is fatal: it makes A* too pessimistic, so it never looks, and never discovers anything. Being too keen costs you time; being too dismissive costs you the answer.

7. Admissibility

Definition — admissible (optimistic) heuristic

A heuristic $h$ is admissible if for every node $n$ $$0 \;\le\; h(n) \;\le\; h^*(n),$$ where $h^*(n)$ is the true cost of a cheapest path from $n$ to a nearest goal (and $h^*(n) = \infty$ if no goal is reachable from $n$).

Both inequalities carry weight. The right one says never overestimate. The left one says never hand the search a negative estimate, which would make $f$ look cheaper than a path that already exists and breaks the same guarantees from the other side.

The word the course uses for this is optimistic, and it is the right word. An admissible heuristic is the friend who always says “oh, it's only about twenty minutes away” — sometimes right, never pessimistic, occasionally wildly hopeful. $h \equiv 0$ is the most optimistic heuristic of all (“we're basically there”), and it is admissible for every problem, which is why UCS is a special case of A*.

Intuition — why optimism is the safe direction

A* only ever discards a plan by saying “its $f$ is too big”. If every $f$ is an underestimate of the truth, then a plan whose $f$ is too big has a truth that is even bigger — so discarding it was safe. Admissibility is precisely the licence to trust A*'s dismissals. An optimistic estimate can only ever cause A* to look somewhere it did not need to; it can never cause A* to skip somewhere it did need to.

Two sanity conditions fall straight out of the definition and you should check them reflexively:

  • $h(\text{goal}) = 0$. At a goal, $h^*=0$, so $0 \le h \le 0$ forces $h = 0$. Every proof below uses this line.
  • Admissibility is a claim about every state, not most states. One overestimating node is enough to destroy optimality, as Figure 4 shows with exactly one guilty node.
Example — the ones you already believe
  • Straight-line distance on a road map: admissible, because a road between two towns cannot be shorter than the segment joining them. (The slide's “15” arrow and the Romania map's “366” at Arad are both instances of this.)
  • Manhattan distance in a 4-connected grid with unit step costs: admissible, because every move changes exactly one coordinate by exactly 1, so you need at least $|\Delta x| + |\Delta y|$ moves — and walls only make it worse.
  • Euclidean distance in that same grid: still admissible (it is $\le$ Manhattan), but weaker — it underestimates by more, so it prunes less. See §15 on dominance.
  • Manhattan distance when diagonal moves cost 1: not admissible. One diagonal move covers $|\Delta x| + |\Delta y| = 2$ of Manhattan distance at a cost of 1, so the estimate can be double the truth. Always re-check admissibility when you change the action set.

And a sentence from the slides that is easy to skim past and is really career advice: “coming up with admissible heuristics is most of what's involved in using A* in practice.” The algorithm is twenty lines and will not change for the rest of your life. The heuristic is where the work is. §14 is about manufacturing them.

8. The optimality of A* tree search — the full blocking argument

The slides state the theorem, draw the picture, and write “TODO” where the three steps go. Here are the three steps, with every justification spelled out.

Theorem — A* tree search is optimal

If $h$ is admissible, then A* tree search (goal test on pop, frontier ordered by $f = g+h$) returns an optimal solution.

Setup, exactly as on the slide. Assume:

  • $A$ is an optimal goal node — a node whose state is a goal, reached along a cheapest possible path, so $g(A) = C^*$, the optimal solution cost;
  • $B$ is a suboptimal goal node — its state is a goal too, but $g(B) > C^*$;
  • $h$ is admissible.

Claim: $A$ will be chosen for expansion (popped off the frontier) before $B$.

Why that claim is enough: A* returns the first goal node it pops. If every optimal goal node beats every suboptimal goal node to the front of the queue, then the first goal popped cannot be suboptimal. So the returned solution is optimal. All the work is in the claim.

The picture: blocking

Figure 5 · The blocking argument — an ancestor $n$ of $A$ stands in front of $B$ in the queue
$n$ is any node on the frontier that lies on an optimal path to $A$ — possibly the start state, possibly $A$ itself. The argument shows $n$ always jumps the queue ahead of $B$, so $B$ can never be popped while any such $n$ is waiting. $B$ is blocked.

Why there is always such an $n$

This is the step the slide compresses into one parenthesis — “(maybe the start state; maybe $A$ itself!)” — and it deserves a paragraph, because the whole proof rests on it.

Fix an optimal path $S = n_0 \to n_1 \to \dots \to n_k = A$. I claim that at every moment before $A$ is popped, at least one $n_i$ on that path is sitting on the frontier. Induct on time:

  • Initially the frontier is $\{S\} = \{n_0\}$, and $n_0$ is on the path. ✔
  • Inductively, suppose some $n_i$ is on the frontier. The only way it leaves the frontier is by being popped and expanded. But expanding $n_i$ generates all its children, one of which is $n_{i+1}$ — which is therefore pushed onto the frontier at that very moment. So the property is restored immediately, with $i$ increased by 1. ✔
  • The index can only climb to $k$, at which point the frontier node is $A$ — and we have stopped, because popping $A$ is the event we were waiting for. ✔

So the frontier always contains an unexpanded “front” of the optimal path. That is the general fact that the frontier separates the explored region from the unexplored region: you cannot get from $S$ to $A$ without crossing it.

Step 1: $f(n) \le f(A)$

Proof — step 1

$$ \begin{aligned} f(n) &= g(n) + h(n) &&\text{definition of the $f$-cost} \\ &\le g(n) + h^*(n) &&\text{admissibility of $h$: } h(n) \le h^*(n) \\ &= g(A) &&\text{$n$ lies on an optimal path to $A$} \\ &= f(A) &&\text{$h = 0$ at a goal, so } f(A) = g(A) + 0 \end{aligned} $$

Line by line.

  • Line 1 is just the definition; nothing is assumed.
  • Line 2 replaces the guess $h(n)$ by the truth $h^*(n)$. The inequality points this way — and only this way — because $h$ is admissible. This is the only place admissibility is used, and it is the hinge of the whole theorem.
  • Line 3 is the subtle one. $g(n)$ is the cost from $S$ to $n$ along the optimal path we fixed, and $h^*(n)$ is the cheapest cost from $n$ onward. Since $n$ sits on an optimal path to $A$, the cheapest completion from $n$ is the rest of that very path, so $g(n) + h^*(n)$ is the cost of a complete optimal path: $g(n) + h^*(n) = C^* = g(A)$. (If $n$ were an arbitrary node this line would fail — it is why we insisted $n$ be on the optimal path.)
  • Line 4 uses $h(A) = 0$, which we proved is forced by admissibility at any goal node.

In words: the $f$-value of a node on an optimal path never exceeds the cost of that optimal path. An optimistic estimate cannot inflate an honest partial plan past its own true total.

Step 2: $f(A) < f(B)$

Proof — step 2

$$ \begin{aligned} f(A) &= g(A) + h(A) = g(A) &&\text{definition of $f$; } h = 0 \text{ at a goal} \\ f(B) &= g(B) + h(B) = g(B) &&\text{identically, since $B$ is a goal node too} \\ g(A) &< g(B) &&\text{$B$ is suboptimal and $A$ is optimal} \\ \therefore\quad f(A) &< f(B) \end{aligned} $$

Notice there is no heuristic left in this step at all: at goal nodes $f$ collapses to $g$, so comparing two goals by $f$ is comparing two solutions by their real cost. The inequality is strict because “suboptimal” means strictly more expensive than the optimum. (If $g(B) = g(A)$ then $B$ is also optimal, and returning it would be perfectly fine — the theorem only promises optimal cost, not a unique path.)

Step 3: therefore $n$ — and then $A$ — is expanded before $B$

Proof — step 3 and the conclusion

Chain steps 1 and 2: $$f(n) \;\le\; f(A) \;<\; f(B) \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad f(n) < f(B).$$

A* always pops the frontier node of minimum $f$. While both $n$ and $B$ sit on the frontier, $n$'s key is strictly smaller, so $B$ cannot be selected: some strictly better node is always available. $n$ is expanded first.

Now iterate. Expanding $n$ puts the next node of the optimal path, $n_{i+1}$, on the frontier — and $n_{i+1}$ is also an ancestor of $A$ on the optimal path, so the identical argument applies to it. One after another, all ancestors of $A$ are expanded before $B$. The chain is finite and ends at $A$ itself, so $A$ is expanded before $B$.

$B$ was an arbitrary suboptimal goal node, so no suboptimal goal can be popped while any optimal goal's path is still pending. Hence the first goal node A* pops has cost $C^*$. A* tree search is optimal. $\blacksquare$

What the argument is really doing, in plain English

Think of the frontier as a queue at a ticket window where the number on your ticket is $f$, and lower numbers are served first. The bad goal $B$ is standing in that queue holding a ticket numbered $g(B)$ — its true, honest, inflated cost, because heuristics vanish at goals and cannot hide it.

Meanwhile the good path to $A$ always has somebody standing in the same queue — you cannot reach $A$ without crossing the frontier, and whenever that person is served, the next person on the good path immediately takes their place. And that person's ticket number is at most $C^*$, because an optimistic heuristic cannot inflate a partial optimal plan beyond the true cost of the whole thing.

So there is a permanent relay of people holding tickets $\le C^* < g(B)$ standing in front of $B$. $B$ never gets served until the relay has run all the way to $A$ — and the moment $A$ is served, the search is over. That relay is what “blocking” means.

Common mistake — three, actually
  1. Goal-testing on insertion. If you return a goal the moment you generate it, the blocking argument evaporates: $B$ never had to win the queue. Test on pop. In Figure 4's counterexample and in Guided problem 1 this is the difference between right and wrong answers.
  2. Assuming $n$ can be any node. Step 1's third line needs $n$ on an optimal path to $A$. For a general node, $g(n) + h^*(n)$ is the cost of the best path through $n$, which may be worse than $C^*$.
  3. Thinking this proves anything about graph search. It does not — the proof never mentions an explored set, and §9 shows an admissible heuristic that breaks graph search on a four-node example.
In step 1, where exactly is admissibility used?
Why: admissibility is precisely the statement $h(n) \le h^*(n)$, which is what licenses that one inequality. Everything else is definitions and the assumption that $B$ is suboptimal. That is why an inadmissible heuristic kills the theorem instantly: without that line, $f(n)$ can exceed $f(A)$, the relay in front of $B$ collapses, and Figure 4 happens. (Option B is true, but it is an consequence of admissibility at goals, used in the last line, not the inequality that does the work.)

9. A* graph search, and why admissibility is not enough

Everything above was about tree search: no explored set, states may be re-generated as often as they like. Real implementations use graph search — keep an explored (closed) set, never expand the same state twice — because otherwise a maze with a cycle in it will drown you. The slide's definition is deliberately minimal:

  • A* tree search = the tree search algorithm from Chapter 1, frontier = priority queue keyed by $f(n) = g(n)+h(n)$.
  • A* graph search = the UCS graph search algorithm from Chapter 1, frontier = priority queue keyed by $f(n) = g(n)+h(n)$.

One word changed and the optimality theorem broke. Here is the four-node counterexample that shows it.

Why this is not the same bug as Chapter 1's

In UCS you met a node getting replaced on the frontier by a cheaper route to the same state, and everything was fine. The replacement rule only works on nodes that are still on the frontier. Once a state is popped and moved to the explored set, graph search regards it as settled: “I already found the best way here.”

For UCS that belief is true. UCS pops in increasing $g$, so the first time a state is popped, its $g$ is the minimum possible — nothing cheaper can appear later. For A*, the pop order is by $f$, and $f$ mixes in a guess. Here A* popped $C$ with $g=3$ because $h(C)=1$ made that route look finished ($f=4$), while the genuinely better route to $C$ was still hiding behind $A$, whose $h(A) = 4$ made it look expensive ($f = 5$). The heuristic told A* to settle $C$ too early.

Key idea

Admissibility guarantees that A* pops goal nodes in a safe order. It says nothing about the order in which A* pops ordinary nodes. Graph search's closed set is a bet that the first pop of every state is its best pop — and that bet needs a stronger property than admissibility. The property is called consistency.

Two other repairs exist, and you should know they exist so that you recognise them in code you read: beyond syllabus (i) reopening — when a closed state is rediscovered with smaller $g$, pull it back out of the explored set and re-push it; this restores optimality with a merely admissible $h$ at the cost of possibly re-expanding states many times. (ii) Run tree search and eat the duplicates. The course's route is different and better: fix the heuristic instead.

10. Consistency (the triangle inequality)

Admissibility constrains $h$ at each node relative to the goal. Consistency constrains $h$ across each edge, so that the estimate cannot drop faster than you can actually travel.

Definition — consistent (monotone) heuristic

$h$ is consistent if for every node $A$ and every successor $C$ of $A$, $$h(A) - h(C) \;\le\; \text{cost}(A \to C), \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad h(A) \;\le\; \text{cost}(A, C) + h(C),$$ and $h(g) = 0$ at every goal $g$.

In the slide's words: the “heuristic step cost” must be $\le$ the actual cost, for each step. The second form is a triangle inequality — “kind of”, as the slide says — with the three sides being $A \to$ goal (estimated), $A \to C$ (real), $C \to$ goal (estimated).

Analogy

$h$ is a sign at each junction reading “Bucharest: about 190 km”. Consistency says the signs must be mutually believable: if you drive 80 km down one road and the next sign claims “Bucharest: about 60 km”, the pair of signs is lying, because together they assert you covered 130 km of distance-to-go by driving 80 km. A consistent set of signs never decreases by more than the distance you actually drove.

Where the analogy breaks: signs may increase by as much as they like — driving 80 km away from the goal may legally raise the estimate by 80. The constraint is one-sided.

Consequence 1 — $f$ never decreases along a path

Proof — consistency $\Rightarrow$ $f$ is non-decreasing along any path

Let $C$ be a successor of $A$ on some path, so that $g(C) = g(A) + \text{cost}(A,C)$ (the definition of $g$ along a branch). Then $$ \begin{aligned} f(C) &= g(C) + h(C) &&\text{definition of $f$}\\ &= g(A) + \text{cost}(A,C) + h(C) &&\text{definition of $g$ along the branch}\\ &\ge g(A) + h(A) &&\text{consistency: } \text{cost}(A,C) + h(C) \ge h(A)\\ &= f(A). &&\text{definition of $f$} \end{aligned} $$ So $f(C) \ge f(A)$: as you walk down any branch of the search tree, $f$ is monotone non-decreasing. (Hence the other name for consistency: monotonicity.) $\blacksquare$

Why that matters so much: if $f$ never decreases along a path, then the $f$-value of the node A* pops never decreases over time either. A* sweeps outward in bands of increasing $f$ — exactly the way UCS sweeps outward in bands of increasing $g$ — and the first time a state is popped, its $g$ is already optimal. Nothing better can arrive later, because anything arriving later arrives with an $f$ that is at least as large. That is precisely the bet the explored set makes, so:

Consequence 2 — A* graph search is optimal when $h$ is consistent

With consistency, closing a state is safe: the route by which a state was first popped is a cheapest route to it. The discard in row 3 of the Figure 6 trace can then never throw away anything valuable, because a discarded rediscovery is never an improvement.

Sanity-check Figure 6 against the definition: the edge $A \to C$ has $h(A) - h(C) = 4 - 1 = 3$, while $\text{cost}(A,C) = 1$. Since $3 > 1$, that edge violates consistency — and it is exactly the edge whose rediscovery got discarded. The counterexample was not bad luck; it was built on the single inconsistent edge.

Consistency implies admissibility

Proof — every consistent heuristic is admissible

Take any node $n$ and let $n = m_0 \to m_1 \to \dots \to m_k = \gamma$ be a cheapest path from $n$ to a nearest goal $\gamma$, so that by definition $$h^*(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} \text{cost}(m_i, m_{i+1}).$$ Induct on $k$, the number of steps on that path.

Base case $k = 0$. Then $n$ is itself a goal, $h^*(n) = 0$, and consistency requires $h(n) = 0$. So $h(n) \le h^*(n)$. ✔

Inductive step. Suppose the claim holds for every node whose cheapest path to a goal has $k-1$ steps. The suffix $m_1 \to \dots \to m_k$ is a cheapest path from $m_1$ to a goal (a suffix of a cheapest path is a cheapest path — otherwise splice in the cheaper suffix and beat $h^*(n)$), and it has $k-1$ steps, so the hypothesis gives $h(m_1) \le h^*(m_1)$. Then $$ \begin{aligned} h(n) &\le \text{cost}(n, m_1) + h(m_1) &&\text{consistency across the first edge}\\ &\le \text{cost}(n, m_1) + h^*(m_1) &&\text{inductive hypothesis}\\ &= h^*(n). &&\text{the path is cheapest, so its cost splits this way} \end{aligned} $$ ✔ By induction, $h(n) \le h^*(n)$ for every $n$; together with $h \ge 0$ that is admissibility. $\blacksquare$

The converse is false, and you have already seen the witness: Figure 6's $h = (2,4,1,0)$ is admissible and inconsistent. So consistent is strictly stronger than admissible.

Intuition — why the implication goes that way

Consistency is a local promise: “I never shrink faster than you can walk.” Admissibility is a global promise: “I never exceed the true remaining distance.” Chaining a local promise all the way to the goal produces the global one — each step gives up at most the real cost of that step, and after the last step the estimate is 0, so the total given up is at most the real total. Going the other direction fails because a global bound says nothing about how wildly the estimate may bounce around between two neighbours.

Suppose $h(n)$ is the exact optimal cost of $n$ in a relaxed problem: same states, same or cheaper edges, possibly extra edges. Five lines worth annotating, because each one is an exam question waiting to happen:

  • choose a node and remove it from the frontier — “choose” means choose the minimum-priority node. This is the only place the priority is consulted, and it is why swapping the key is the only edit needed.
  • if the node contains a goal state then return sits after the pop and before the expansion. Goal-test-on-pop. Move it up into the child loop and both algorithms lose optimality.
  • add the node state to the explored set is the line that makes these graph search. Delete it (and the corresponding membership check) and you have tree search, which is what §8's proof is about.
  • if the child state is not already in the frontier or explored set — state, not node. Two different nodes with the same state are the thing we are policing.
  • else if the child is already in the frontier with higher f(n) then replace that frontier node with child — the repair branch. It handles only the frontier case. There is deliberately no branch for “already in the explored set with higher $f$”, and that missing branch is exactly the bug in Figure 6. Replacement also rewrites the node's parent pointer, or the recovered path will not match the recovered cost.
Common mistake — comparing $f$ when you should compare $g$

In the A* version the replacement test is written on $f$. For two nodes with the same state that is equivalent to testing $g$, because they share the same $h$: $f_1 > f_2 \iff g_1 + h > g_2 + h \iff g_1 > g_2$. So both phrasings are correct here — but only because the states match. Never compare $f$ across different states to decide which path is cheaper; that comparison is meaningless.

13. UCS vs A* contours

The clearest mental image of what the heuristic buys you is the shape of the region the algorithm expands.

Figure 7 · Cost contours — UCS expands circles around the start, A* expands ellipses stretched toward the goal
These are not a cartoon — they are the exact level sets. UCS expands every node with $g \le C^*$: a ball around the start. A* expands every node with $f = g+h < C^*$; with a perfect Euclidean $h$ that set is $\{p : d(\text{start},p) + d(p,\text{goal}) \le C^*\}$, which is the textbook definition of an ellipse with foci at the start and the goal. The bolder outer curve in each panel is the $C^*$ contour, the boundary of what must be expanded.

Now you can read off the entire story of the heuristic's quality as a geometric parameter:

  • $h \equiv 0$: the ellipse's minor axis is as long as its major axis — the ellipse is the circle, and A* is UCS.
  • A weak but admissible $h$: a fat ellipse. Fewer nodes than UCS, still a lot.
  • A strong admissible $h$: a thin cigar hugging the straight line between start and goal.
  • $h = h^*$: the ellipse collapses onto the segment itself — A* expands only nodes on optimal paths. This is the best any heuristic can do.

The word hedges in the slide is the important one. A* does not expand only the straight line, even with a good heuristic, and it must not: the sideways bulge is exactly the set of alternatives it is obliged to rule out before it can honestly claim optimality. Greedy skips the bulge, which is why it is faster and why it can be wrong. The bulge is the price of the proof. Go back to Figure 3 and run A* once more with this in mind — the fringe it fills in on either side of the corridor is the bulge.

14. Creating heuristics: relaxed problems

You now know what makes a heuristic legal. Where do you get one? The slides give the single most useful recipe in the whole search unit:

The recipe — relaxation

Admissible heuristics are usually the exact solution costs of relaxed problems, where you delete constraints so that new actions become available.

Why it always works: any solution to the real problem is still a legal solution to the relaxed problem (you removed rules, you did not add any), so the relaxed optimum can only be cheaper. Therefore $$h(n) \;=\; \text{optimal cost from } n \text{ in the relaxed problem} \;\le\; h^*(n),$$ which is admissibility, obtained for free — you never have to check it case by case. And by the argument in §11, such an $h$ is normally consistent too.

The Romania heuristic is exactly this. Relaxation: let the car drive in a straight line across fields, ignoring roads. The optimal cost of that relaxed problem is the straight-line distance — 366 km from Arad — and it is computable with Pythagoras instead of search. The trick is to relax just enough that the relaxed problem becomes trivially solvable, while still capturing something real about the difficulty.

Example: the 8-puzzle

The slide poses five questions about the 8-puzzle and leaves them for the poll. Here are all five, answered.

The 8-puzzle as a formal search problem
  • What are the states? An arrangement of the eight numbered tiles plus the blank in the nine cells of a $3\times3$ board — i.e. a permutation of the nine symbols $\{1,\dots,8,\square\}$ over nine positions.
  • How many states? $9! = 362{,}880$ arrangements. But only half of them — 181,440 — are reachable from any given start, because a legal move always swaps the blank with a neighbour, and sliding the blank around and back always changes tile-permutation parity in lockstep with the blank's own displacement. The state space splits into two components that never communicate: if someone pries out two tiles and swaps them, the puzzle becomes unsolvable, and no amount of search will tell you otherwise — it will just exhaust the reachable half.
  • What are the actions? Move the blank up, down, left or right (equivalently: slide the adjacent tile into the hole). Defining actions on the blank rather than on the tiles gives a branching factor of at most 4 instead of 8, for the same state space — a free win, and a good habit.
  • How many actions from the start state? It depends only on where the blank is: a corner blank has 2 legal moves, an edge blank 3, and the centre blank 4. In the standard start state below the blank is in the centre, so 4. (Average branching factor over the whole space is about 3.)
  • What should the step costs be? $c = 1$ per move. We are minimising the number of moves, so making every move cost one unit makes path cost equal number of moves. With unit costs, $h^*$ is measured in moves and every heuristic below must be too.

8-puzzle heuristic I — misplaced tiles

$h_{\text{TILES}}$

$h_{\text{TILES}}(n) = $ the number of tiles that are not in their goal position (never counting the blank).

On the start state above, every one of the eight tiles is somewhere it should not be, so $h_{\text{TILES}}(\text{start}) = 8$.

Why is it admissible? Each move slides exactly one tile into a new cell. So every misplaced tile requires at least one move of its own before the puzzle can be solved, and no single move can fix two tiles at once. Therefore the number of remaining moves is at least the number of misplaced tiles: $h_{\text{TILES}} \le h^*$. ✔

Which relaxation is it solving? Delete both rules about where a tile may go: a tile may be picked up and teleported directly to its goal square, from anywhere, in one move. In that fantasy puzzle the optimal solution is obviously “one teleport per misplaced tile” = 8 moves. That exact relaxed cost is $h_{\text{TILES}}$ — which is why admissibility did not have to be argued case by case.

8-puzzle heuristic II — total Manhattan distance

The slide asks: what if we had an easier 8-puzzle where any tile could slide any direction at any time, ignoring other tiles? Relax only one rule — the one that says a tile may only move into the blank — and keep the rule that a tile moves one square at a time, orthogonally.

$h_{\text{MANHATTAN}}$

$h_{\text{MANHATTAN}}(n) = \sum_{\text{tiles } t} \big( |r_t - r_t^{\text{goal}}| + |c_t - c_t^{\text{goal}}| \big)$ — the total Manhattan distance of every tile from where it belongs.

On the start state, tile by tile in numeric order: $3+1+2+2+2+3+3+2 = \mathbf{18}$, exactly the slide's “$h(\text{start}) = 3+1+2+\dots = 18$”.

Why is it admissible? A tile at Manhattan distance $d$ from its home needs at least $d$ moves of its own, because one move changes one of its coordinates by one. Summing over tiles, and using again that each move moves only one tile, gives $h_{\text{MANHATTAN}} \le h^*$. ✔ Note both facts are needed: “$\ge d$ moves per tile” and “no move helps two tiles”. Drop the second and you could double-count.

And $h_{\text{MANHATTAN}} \ge h_{\text{TILES}}$ always, because a misplaced tile contributes at least 1 to the sum and a correctly placed one contributes 0. Keep that in mind for §15 — it is the definition of dominance, and it is why the numbers below come out the way they do.

Guided problem 3Design a heuristic by relaxation, for a problem you have not seen

Problem. Pacman is in a maze with walls. There are $k$ dots left. A state is (Pacman's position, which dots remain). Actions are N/S/E/W into a non-wall square, each costing 1; a dot is eaten automatically by stepping on it. The goal test is “no dots remain”. Design an admissible heuristic, argue why it is admissible, and say which relaxation it comes from. Then find a better one.

Step 1 — First, list the constraints you could delete

Relaxation is a menu, so write the menu down. The real problem says: (i) you must move one square at a time; (ii) you cannot pass through walls; (iii) you must visit every remaining dot; (iv) you can only be in one place at a time.

Each rule you delete makes a different, easier problem — and its exact cost is a candidate heuristic. Deleting nothing gives $h = h^*$ (perfect, unaffordable). Deleting everything gives $h = 0$ (free, useless). The craft is in between.

Step 2 — Candidate A: delete rule (iii) down to “eat one dot”

Relaxation: you only have to reach the farthest remaining dot; the others vanish on their own.

$h_A = \max_{\text{dots } d} \text{mazeDistance}(\text{pacman}, d)$.

Admissible? Yes. Any real plan must at some point step onto that farthest dot, and getting there costs at least its maze distance. So $h_A \le h^*$. ✔

Use maze distance (a small BFS that respects walls), not Manhattan — it is larger, still admissible, and therefore better (§15). If you cannot afford the BFS, Manhattan distance is a legal, weaker fallback.

A near-miss worth naming: using the distance to the nearest dot is also admissible but much weaker; using the sum over all dots is not admissible at all — one walk can collect many dots, so the sum wildly overestimates, and A* will happily return garbage.

Step 3 — Candidate B: delete rules (i) and (ii)

Relaxation: Pacman teleports to any square he likes, but still must land on each dot once.

$h_B = $ the number of remaining dots, $k$.

Admissible? Yes: each move eats at most one dot (you land on one square), so at least $k$ moves remain. ✔ It costs $O(1)$ to compute if you keep a counter in the state.

Notice $h_A$ and $h_B$ measure different things — distance versus quantity — and neither dominates the other. One dot far away: $h_A$ is big, $h_B = 1$. Fifty dots all adjacent: $h_B = 50$, $h_A$ is small. This is the exact situation §15 tells you what to do about.

Answer & the takeaway

Answer: $h(n) = \max\big(h_A(n),\, h_B(n)\big)$ — the larger of “maze distance to the farthest remaining dot” and “number of remaining dots”. It is admissible because the max of admissible heuristics is admissible (§15 proves it), and it dominates both of its parts, so A* with it expands no more nodes than with either alone.

beyond syllabus Stronger still: relax rule (iv) only partially and take $h = \text{(distance to the nearest dot)} + \text{(weight of a minimum spanning tree over the remaining dots, using maze distances)}$. Still admissible — any tour that visits all dots contains a spanning connected subgraph of them — and much tighter. This is the standard answer to the Berkeley “eat all the food” project.

The takeaway, and the procedure to use in an exam: (1) write the problem's rules as a list; (2) cross one out; (3) ask “can I now compute the optimal cost in closed form or with a trivial algorithm?”; (4) if yes, that cost is your heuristic and it is admissible by construction; (5) if two candidates disagree about which situations are hard, take the max.

15. Dominance, and combining heuristics

Definition — dominance

For two heuristics, $h_a$ dominates $h_c$ — written $h_a \ge h_c$ — if $$\forall n:\quad h_a(n) \;\ge\; h_c(n).$$ Read it as: $h_a$ is everywhere at least as well informed as $h_c$. (The $\forall n$ is doing real work: being bigger on most nodes is not dominance and buys no guarantee.)

The slide's summary: roughly speaking, larger is better — as long as both are admissible. Here is why, and it is worth being precise because “larger is better” sounds like it should be “smaller is safer”.

Why larger is better

With a consistent heuristic, A* must expand every node with $f(n) < C^*$, i.e. every node with $g(n) + h(n) < C^*$, and may skip the rest. Now suppose $h_a \ge h_c$, both admissible. If a node satisfies $g(n) + h_a(n) < C^*$ then it also satisfies $g(n) + h_c(n) < C^*$, since $h_c \le h_a$. So $$\{\text{nodes A*}_{h_a} \text{ must expand}\} \;\subseteq\; \{\text{nodes A*}_{h_c} \text{ must expand}\}.$$ The better-informed search's workload is a subset of the worse-informed one's. Larger admissible $h$ ⟹ more nodes pruned ⟹ never more work (up to tie-breaking). $\blacksquare$

And it does not cost correctness, because admissibility caps how large $h$ may get: the ceiling is $h^*$. “Larger is better” only holds beneath that ceiling; above it, larger means wrong, which is Figure 4.

The two extremes named in the slides:

  • The zero heuristic is pretty bad. “What does A* do with $h=0$?” — it becomes uniform-cost search exactly: the priority is $g + 0 = g$. It is still complete and optimal, and it is dominated by every other admissible heuristic, so it does the most work any admissible A* can do. It is the floor.
  • The exact heuristic is pretty good, but usually too expensive. $h = h^*$ dominates everything admissible (nothing may exceed $h^*$), and A* with it expands only nodes on optimal paths. The catch is circular: computing $h^*(n)$ means solving the search problem from $n$. If you could do that cheaply you would not be running A*. It is the ceiling, and it is unreachable by construction.

Every useful heuristic lives strictly between those two, and the design question is always the same trade: a heuristic closer to $h^*$ prunes more nodes but costs more per node. A heuristic that halves the node count and triples the per-node cost is a loss.

When neither dominates: take the max

The max trick

Given admissible $h_a$ and $h_b$ where neither dominates the other, define $$h(n) \;=\; \max\big(h_a(n),\, h_b(n)\big).$$ The max of admissible heuristics is admissible, and it dominates both.

Proof

Admissible. Fix any node $n$. The value $\max(h_a(n), h_b(n))$ equals either $h_a(n)$ or $h_b(n)$ — whichever is larger — and each of those is $\le h^*(n)$ by the admissibility of its own heuristic. So the max is $\le h^*(n)$. Non-negativity is immediate, since the max of two non-negative numbers is non-negative. Hence $0 \le h(n) \le h^*(n)$. ✔

Dominates both. By definition of the maximum, $\max(h_a(n), h_b(n)) \ge h_a(n)$ and $\ge h_b(n)$, for every $n$. That is exactly $h \ge h_a$ and $h \ge h_b$. ✔ $\blacksquare$

beyond syllabus The max of two consistent heuristics is also consistent. Fix an edge $A \to C$ and suppose the max at $A$ is attained by $h_a$. Then $h(A) = h_a(A) \le \text{cost}(A,C) + h_a(C) \le \text{cost}(A,C) + h(C)$, using consistency of $h_a$ and then $h_a(C) \le h(C)$. Symmetrically if $h_b$ attains it. So you may combine consistent heuristics freely without losing graph-search optimality.

Common mistake — averaging, or adding

$\tfrac12(h_a + h_b)$ is admissible but pointless: it is dominated by the max, so it does strictly more work for the same guarantee. And $h_a + h_b$ is not admissible in general — two admissible estimates added together can easily exceed $h^*$ (take $h_a = h_b = h^*$; the sum is $2h^*$). Max is the only combinator that is both safe and never worse than its inputs.

$h_1$ and $h_2$ are admissible. Which of these is guaranteed admissible and at least as informed as both?
Why: the max never exceeds $h^*$ (each argument is capped by $h^*$) and by definition sits above both inputs. The sum can shoot past $h^*$ and destroy optimality. The min is admissible but is dominated by both inputs — it is the worst legal combination, and it always makes A* do more work. The average is admissible but dominated by the max. When in doubt: push $h$ as high as you can without passing $h^*$.
Your A* graph search with an admissible-but-inconsistent $h$ returns a suboptimal path. Which single change is guaranteed to fix optimality without changing $h$?
Why: §8's theorem needs only admissibility — but it is a theorem about tree search. Removing the explored set restores exactly the hypotheses of that proof, at the cost of re-expanding repeated states (and risking non-termination on infinite graphs with cycles). Testing goals at generation makes things strictly worse, and is how you lose optimality even with a perfect heuristic. Tie-breaking changes which optimal answer you get, never whether you get one. Adding a constant to $h$ breaks $h(\text{goal}) = 0$ and can push $h$ above $h^*$, i.e. it can make things inadmissible. The other legal fix is to allow reopening closed nodes — but that is a change to the algorithm, and it is not in the slides' pseudocode.

16. Recap

The whole chapter in twelve lines
  • A heuristic $h(n)$ estimates the cost from $n$'s state to a nearest goal. It is problem-specific and you write it.
  • Greedy orders the frontier by $h$: fast, incomplete as tree search, never optimal.
  • A* orders by $f(n) = g(n) + h(n)$ — backward cost plus estimated forward cost — i.e. by the estimated total cost of the best solution through $n$.
  • Goal-test on pop, never on generation.
  • Admissible: $0 \le h(n) \le h^*(n)$. Optimistic. Never overestimate.
  • A* tree search is optimal when $h$ is admissible — by blocking: an ancestor $n$ of the optimal goal $A$ is always on the frontier with $f(n) \le f(A) < f(B)$, so the suboptimal goal $B$ can never be popped first.
  • Consistent: $h(A) - h(C) \le \text{cost}(A \to C)$ for every edge. Triangle inequality. Implies admissible; the converse fails.
  • Consistency $\Rightarrow$ $f$ never decreases along a path $\Rightarrow$ the first pop of a state is its cheapest $\Rightarrow$ A* graph search is optimal.
  • UCS is A* with $h = 0$, which is admissible and consistent — so UCS is optimal in both settings, and it is the most work an admissible A* can do.
  • UCS expands circles; A* expands ellipses squashed toward the goal. The sideways bulge is the price of the optimality proof.
  • Build heuristics by relaxation — delete a rule, solve the easier problem exactly. Admissible (and usually consistent) by construction. 8-puzzle: $h_{\text{TILES}} = 8$ (teleporting tiles), $h_{\text{MANHATTAN}} = 18$ (tiles pass through each other).
  • Dominance: larger is better while still $\le h^*$. If neither of two dominates, take the max — admissible, and dominates both.
