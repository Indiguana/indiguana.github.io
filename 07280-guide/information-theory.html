Entropy, conditional entropy & mutual information

After this page you can compute entropy, conditional entropy and information gain by hand, prove why gain is never negative, and run BestAttribute on a table of data without a calculator.

Where we left off: a criterion that cannot see progress

Chapter 6 ended with a dataset on which splitting on $a$, splitting on $b$, and not splitting at all all produced a training error of exactly $2/8$. The error-rate criterion had no opinion, even though split $b$ purified half the dataset and split $a$ accomplished literally nothing.

The diagnosis was precise. Training error only asks which label wins the vote in each child. It is blind to by how much. A child that goes from a 50/50 coin flip to a 75/25 near-certainty has made enormous progress, but the majority vote did not flip, so the error count did not move.

What we actually want to measure is uncertainty, on a continuous scale, so that a split that makes the labels more predictable gets credit even when no prediction changes yet. That measure exists, it is unique up to a constant, and it comes from a surprisingly humble place: a parlour game.

Key idea

The whole chapter is one sentence: uncertainty about a random variable = the average number of yes/no questions you need to pin it down. Everything else — entropy, conditional entropy, mutual information, information gain — is bookkeeping around that one sentence.

The 20-questions game

Here is the professor's setup, verbatim in substance:

Example

There are $128$ students in the class. I choose one of them uniformly at random — every student equally likely, probability $1/128$ each. You may ask me any yes/no questions you like, and then you must name the student I chose. How many questions do you need?

Notice what "any yes/no question" means. You are not restricted to "Is it Alice?". You may ask "Is the student's surname in the first half of the alphabetical roster?" — any question whose answer is a single bit about the chosen student.

The answer, and both halves of the proof

The answer is $\mathbf{7}$, and

$$7 \;=\; \log_2 128 \;=\; \log_2 \frac{1}{1/128} \;=\; \log_2 \frac{1}{P(\text{any particular student})}.$$

That has to be proved in two directions: $7$ questions suffice, and $6$ questions do not.

Proof — 7 questions suffice

Line the $128$ students up in any fixed order you and I both know (say, alphabetically), numbered $0$ through $127$. Keep a set $C$ of "candidates still possible"; initially $|C| = 128$.

Halving strategy. At each step, split $C$ into two halves of equal size and ask "Is the chosen student in the first half?" Whatever the answer, you discard exactly half the candidates:

$$128 \to 64 \to 32 \to 16 \to 8 \to 4 \to 2 \to 1.$$

Count the arrows: there are $7$ of them, because $128 = 2^7$. After $7$ questions $|C| = 1$ and you name that student with certainty. The strategy never needs more than $7$ and never fewer, so the average is exactly $7$. $\blacksquare$

Proof — 6 questions cannot be enough (counting argument)

Fix any strategy that asks at most $k$ yes/no questions. Run it. The only information you ever receive is the sequence of answers, which is a string of $k$ bits. There are at most $2^k$ such strings.

Your final guess is a function of that string alone — nothing else reached you. So your strategy can output at most $2^k$ distinct guesses across all possible plays of the game.

With $k = 6$: at most $2^6 = 64$ distinct guesses. But there are $128$ students, each of which I might have chosen. At least $128 - 64 = 64$ students are never guessed by your strategy, and each of those has probability $1/128$ of being the one I picked. So

$$P(\text{you are correct}) \;\le\; \frac{64}{128} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}.$$

You fail at least half the time. Hence $6$ questions are not enough, and $7$ is optimal. $\blacksquare$

The same counting works for any $k$: $k$ yes/no answers can distinguish at most $2^k$ outcomes, so distinguishing $n$ equally likely outcomes requires $k \ge \log_2 n$.

Intuition

A yes/no answer is worth one bit, and one bit is exactly the power to cut a set of possibilities in half. To go from $128$ possibilities to $1$ you must halve seven times. That is all $\log_2 128 = 7$ is saying — the logarithm is counting halvings.

Why $\log \frac{1}{p}$, and why the logarithm specifically

The notes make the pivotal observation: the probability of the chosen student was $1/128$, and the answer was $\log_2 \frac{1}{1/128}$. So the fundamental quantity is

$$\boxed{\;\text{surprise}(\text{outcome with probability } p) \;=\; \log \frac{1}{p} \;=\; -\log p.\;}$$

This is called the information content or surprisal of the outcome: how many bits it takes to name it, equivalently how startled you should be to see it. Two reasons the logarithm — and nothing else — belongs here.

Reason 1: a 1-in-$2^k$ event literally costs $k$ bits to name

If an outcome has probability $p = 2^{-k}$ out of a uniform pool, then there are $2^k$ equally likely outcomes, and the counting argument above says you need exactly $k$ yes/no questions. And indeed

$$\log_2 \frac{1}{p} = \log_2 2^{k} = k.$$

So $\log_2(1/p)$ is not an analogy for "number of questions" — on powers of two it is the number of questions.

Reason 2: additivity over independent events

Suppose after picking a student I also flip a fair coin, independently. Now I am describing a pair (student, coin). Intuitively, naming the pair should cost naming the student plus naming the coin: $7 + 1 = 8$ questions. Probabilities multiply for independent events, and the logarithm is exactly the function that turns multiplication into addition:

$$\log \frac{1}{p \cdot q} \;=\; \log \frac{1}{p} + \log \frac{1}{q}.$$ $$\log_2 \frac{1}{\tfrac{1}{128}\cdot\tfrac12} \;=\; \log_2 256 \;=\; 8 \;=\; 7 + 1. \quad\checkmark$$
Analogy

Think of surprisal as the length of the name you'd have to assign an outcome in a code book. Common outcomes get short names ("the", "a"), rare outcomes get long ones ("antidisestablishmentarianism"). If an outcome happens $1$ time in $2^k$, you cannot give it a name shorter than $k$ bits without stealing a name that some other outcome needs.

Where the analogy holds exactly: this is Shannon's source coding theorem, and the average name length is precisely entropy. Where it strains: real code words have integer lengths, so you can only hit entropy exactly when every probability is a power of two — see below.

Averaging: the expected number of questions

Now drop the assumption that I choose uniformly. Let there be $k$ outcomes with probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_k$. Outcome $i$ costs $\log \frac{1}{p_i}$ questions and occurs a $p_i$ fraction of the time, so the expected number of questions is the probability-weighted average of the surprisals:

$$\text{Answer} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \log \frac{1}{p_i}.$$

Sanity check against the uniform case: with $p_i = 1/128$ for all $128$ students,

$$\sum_{i=1}^{128} \frac{1}{128}\log_2 128 \;=\; \log_2 128 \cdot \underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{128}\frac{1}{128}}_{=\,1} \;=\; 7. \quad\checkmark$$

The notes' non-uniform example, computed in full

The notes then change the distribution to

$$p_1 = \tfrac14, \qquad p_2 = p_3 = \cdots = p_7 = \tfrac18, \qquad p_8 = \cdots = p_{128} = 0,$$

with the convention $0 \cdot \log\frac10 := 0$, and assert the answer is $11/4$. Let us check that it is even a probability distribution, then verify the number.

Example — verifying $11/4$, every step

Step 0. It is a distribution. $\tfrac14 + 6 \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac68 = \tfrac28 + \tfrac68 = \tfrac88 = 1.$  (Students $2$ through $7$ is six students, not seven.) ✓

Step 1. Surprisal of student 1. $\displaystyle \log_2\frac{1}{1/4} = \log_2 4 = 2$ bits.

Step 2. Surprisal of students 2–7. $\displaystyle \log_2\frac{1}{1/8} = \log_2 8 = 3$ bits each.

Step 3. Surprisal of students 8–128. These have $p_i = 0$. The convention says the term is $0$ — see the justification below.

Step 4. Weighted sum.

$$\sum_i p_i \log_2\frac{1}{p_i} \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac14 \cdot \log_2 4}_{\text{student 1}} \;+\; \underbrace{6 \cdot \tfrac18 \cdot \log_2 8}_{\text{students 2–7}} \;+\; \underbrace{121 \cdot 0}_{\text{students 8–128}}$$ $$= \tfrac14 \cdot 2 \;+\; 6 \cdot \tfrac18 \cdot 3 \;=\; \tfrac{2}{4} \;+\; 6 \cdot \tfrac38 \;=\; \tfrac12 \;+\; \tfrac{18}{8} \;=\; \tfrac12 + \tfrac94.$$

Step 5. Common denominator. $\displaystyle \tfrac12 + \tfrac94 = \tfrac24 + \tfrac94 = \tfrac{11}{4} = 2.75$ questions on average. ✓  This matches the notes exactly.

Definition

The $0\log\frac10 := 0$ convention. An outcome that never happens should contribute nothing to the average. Formally this is not a fudge, it is the limit:

$$\lim_{p\to 0^+} p \log_2 \frac{1}{p} \;=\; \lim_{p\to 0^+} \frac{-\log_2 p}{1/p} \;\overset{\text{L'Hôpital}}{=}\; \lim_{p\to 0^+} \frac{-1/(p\ln 2)}{-1/p^2} \;=\; \lim_{p\to 0^+} \frac{p}{\ln 2} \;=\; 0.$$

So defining the term as $0$ makes $H$ continuous. The surprisal $\log\frac10 = \infty$ is infinite, but it is multiplied by a probability heading to zero faster, and zero wins.

Does a real question strategy actually achieve $11/4$?

Yes, here — and it is worth seeing why, because it also shows when it fails.

Example — a strategy hitting $2.75$ exactly

Ask: Q1: "Is it student 1?" If yes (prob $\tfrac14$) you are done in $1$ question... but that is not optimal. Do it the other way:

Q1: "Is it student 1 or 2?" — the two halves each carry probability $\tfrac14 + \tfrac18 = \tfrac38$... not a clean half either. The clean strategy pairs probability mass into exact halves:

  • Q1: "Is it in $\{1, 2, 3\}$?"  → mass $\tfrac14+\tfrac18+\tfrac18 = \tfrac12$. Exactly half. ✓
  • If yes — Q2: "Is it student 1?" (mass $\tfrac14$ out of $\tfrac12$ = half of the branch ✓). If yes, done in 2 questions. If no, Q3 separates $2$ from $3$: done in 3.
  • If no — the survivors are $\{4,5,6,7\}$, each with mass $\tfrac18$, i.e. four equally likely outcomes. Two more questions (binary search) pin it down: done in 3.

Cost accounting: student 1 costs $2$ questions with probability $\tfrac14$; students $2$–$7$ cost $3$ questions each with probability $\tfrac18$:

$$\mathbb{E}[\#\text{questions}] = \tfrac14(2) + 6\cdot\tfrac18(3) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac94 = \tfrac{11}{4}. \quad\checkmark$$

Notice the number of questions spent on outcome $i$ came out to exactly $\log_2(1/p_i)$ in every case: $2 = \log_2 4$, $3 = \log_2 8$.

Common mistake

"So entropy is the number of questions." Only when every $p_i$ is a power of two, as above. In general $\log_2(1/p_i)$ is not an integer, and you cannot ask $1.58$ questions.

What is true in general: entropy is a lower bound, and you can always get within one question of it. If $H$ is the entropy and $L^\star$ the expected length of the best strategy,

$$H \;\le\; L^\star \;\lt\; H + 1,$$

achieved by rounding each ideal length up, $\lceil \log_2(1/p_i)\rceil$. Example: three equally likely outcomes have $H = \log_2 3 \approx 1.585$ bits, but any real strategy costs $\tfrac13(1) + \tfrac13(2) + \tfrac13(2) = \tfrac53 \approx 1.667$ questions. The gap is the rounding.

For decision trees none of this matters — we only ever compare entropies, never cash them in for actual questions.

Guided problemEntropy and an optimal question strategy

I pick one of four objects with probabilities $p_1 = \tfrac12$, $p_2 = \tfrac14$, $p_3 = \tfrac18$, $p_4 = \tfrac18$.

(a) Compute the entropy. (b) Design a yes/no question strategy and verify its expected cost equals the entropy. (c) Compare against the "just ask is it object 1? is it object 2? in order" strategy.

Step 1 — Write down the surprisal of each outcome

$\log_2\frac{1}{1/2} = \log_2 2 = 1$;   $\log_2\frac{1}{1/4} = \log_2 4 = 2$;   $\log_2\frac{1}{1/8} = \log_2 8 = 3$ (twice).

All powers of two, so we should expect a strategy to match the bound exactly.

Step 2 — Take the weighted average
$$H = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac14(2) + \tfrac18(3) + \tfrac18(3) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac38 + \tfrac38 = 1 + \tfrac68 = \tfrac{7}{4} = 1.75 \text{ bits}.$$

Check it is a distribution first: $\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18+\tfrac18 = \tfrac48+\tfrac28+\tfrac18+\tfrac18 = 1$ ✓.

Step 3 — Build the strategy by splitting the mass in half each time

Q1: "Is it object 1?" Mass on each side: $\tfrac12$ vs $\tfrac12$ — a perfect half. Yes → done in $1$.

Q2 (given no): "Is it object 2?" Remaining mass is $\tfrac14+\tfrac18+\tfrac18 = \tfrac12$, and object 2 holds $\tfrac14$ of it — half again ✓. Yes → done in $2$.

Q3 (given no again): "Is it object 3?" Two outcomes of mass $\tfrac18$ each — half ✓. Either way, done in $3$.

Cost: $\tfrac12(1)+\tfrac14(2)+\tfrac18(3)+\tfrac18(3) = 1.75$. Identical to $H$. ✓

The rule that made it work: every question split the remaining probability mass exactly in half, so every answer carried a full bit of information. That is the general recipe.

Answer & the takeaway

(a) $H = 7/4 = 1.75$ bits. (b) The strategy above costs exactly $1.75$. (c) Here the naive "is it object 1? is it object 2? …" strategy is the optimal one, because the probabilities were arranged to halve at each step. Change the distribution to uniform ($\tfrac14$ each) and the naive strategy costs $\tfrac14(1)+\tfrac14(2)+\tfrac14(3)+\tfrac14(3) = 2.25$, while balanced binary search costs $2 = \log_2 4 = H$.

Takeaway: low entropy = one outcome hogs the mass = you can find it fast. High entropy = the mass is spread out = every question buys you less. That is exactly the property we want from a splitting criterion.

Entropy: the definition and its properties

Definition

For a discrete distribution $p = (p_1, \dots, p_k)$ over $k$ outcomes, the entropy is

$$H(p) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \log \frac{1}{p_i} \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i \log p_i,$$

with the convention $0\log\frac10 := 0$. Every symbol:

  • $k$ — the number of possible outcomes (for us: the number of class labels).
  • $p_i$ — the probability of outcome $i$; in a decision-tree node, the fraction of examples in that node carrying label $i$.
  • $\log\frac{1}{p_i}$ — the surprisal of outcome $i$: how many bits to name it.
  • The sum — an expectation, $H(p) = \mathbb{E}_{i \sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{1}{p_i}\right]$: the average surprisal.
  • The two forms are the same because $\log\frac1{p} = -\log p$. Both appear in the wild; the $-\sum p\log p$ form is the one you will see on exams.

Units. With $\log_2$, entropy is in bits. With $\ln$, it is in nats ($1$ nat $= 1/\ln 2 \approx 1.4427$ bits). With $\log_{10}$, "bans". The base only rescales everything by a constant, so it never changes which attribute wins a split. Use $\log_2$ in this course — it makes the 20-questions story literal.

Property 1: $H(p) \ge 0$

Proof

Take any term $p_i \log\frac{1}{p_i}$. Since $p_i \in [0,1]$ we have $\frac{1}{p_i} \ge 1$, hence $\log\frac{1}{p_i} \ge \log 1 = 0$. The term is a product of two non-negative numbers, so it is $\ge 0$. A sum of non-negative terms is non-negative. $\blacksquare$

Interpretation: you can never need a negative number of questions. Entropy has no upper limit in general (many outcomes ⟹ large $H$), but it has a hard floor at zero.

Property 2: $H(p) = 0$ if and only if the distribution is deterministic

Proof

($\Leftarrow$) If some $p_j = 1$ and all others are $0$: the $j$ term is $1 \cdot \log\frac11 = 1 \cdot 0 = 0$, and every other term is $0\log\frac10 = 0$ by convention. So $H = 0$.

($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $H = 0$. By Property 1 every term is $\ge 0$, and a sum of non-negative numbers is zero only if every term is zero. So for each $i$, $p_i \log\frac{1}{p_i} = 0$, which forces $p_i = 0$ or $\log\frac1{p_i}=0 \Rightarrow p_i = 1$. So every $p_i \in \{0,1\}$; since they sum to $1$, exactly one is $1$. $\blacksquare$

Decision-tree meaning: $H = 0$ ⟺ the node is pure — every example in it has the same label. Zero questions needed, zero uncertainty, and the recursion stops.

Property 3: $H(p) \le \log k$, with equality exactly for the uniform distribution

Proof (Jensen's inequality)

$\log$ is a concave function (its second derivative $-1/(x^2\ln 2)$ is negative everywhere on $x>0$). Jensen's inequality for a concave $f$ says $\mathbb{E}[f(Z)] \le f(\mathbb{E}[Z])$ — the average of the function is at most the function of the average.

Apply it with $Z = 1/p_i$ where $i \sim p$ (summing only over $i$ with $p_i > 0$, say $m \le k$ of them):

$$H(p) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\log \frac{1}{p_i}\right] \;\le\; \log\left(\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{p_i}\right]\right) = \log\left(\sum_{i : p_i > 0} p_i \cdot \frac{1}{p_i}\right) = \log\left(\sum_{i: p_i>0} 1\right) = \log m \;\le\; \log k.$$

Every step: (1) definition of $H$ as an expectation; (2) Jensen, legal because $\log$ is concave; (3) expand the expectation as a sum, the $p_i$ cancels; (4) count the surviving terms.

Equality. $\log$ is strictly concave, so Jensen is tight only when $Z$ is constant, i.e. $1/p_i$ is the same for all $i$ in the support — a uniform distribution. And the last inequality is tight only when $m = k$, i.e. no outcome has probability zero. So $H = \log k$ exactly when $p$ is uniform over all $k$ outcomes. $\blacksquare$

Meaning: maximum uncertainty is "all outcomes equally likely", and it costs $\log_2 k$ questions — the 20-questions answer from the start of the chapter, now as a theorem. For $k=2$ the cap is $\log_2 2 = 1$ bit.

Property 4: entropy measures uncertainty

Not a theorem so much as the summary of Properties 1–3, and the reason we bothered:

Key idea

More uncertainty $\iff$ more questions needed $\iff$ higher entropy. The scale runs from $0$ (you already know the answer — a pure node) to $\log_2 k$ (total ignorance — a perfectly balanced node). Everything in between is graded continuously, which is precisely what training error refused to do.

A node contains 100 examples: 100 labelled "1" and 0 labelled "0". A second node contains 100 examples: 50 and 50. A third contains 100 examples: 99 and 1. Rank their entropies from largest to smallest.
Why: Entropy depends only on the proportions, never on the counts. Node 2 is the uniform distribution on $k=2$ outcomes, so $H = \log_2 2 = 1$ bit — the maximum possible. Node 3 has $H(0.99) \approx 0.081$ bits: small but strictly positive, because one stray example still leaves some uncertainty. Node 1 is deterministic, so $H = 0$ exactly (Property 2). Hence $1 > 0.081 > 0$.

The binary entropy function

Almost every hand computation in this course has two labels, so it is worth learning one function cold. Write $p$ for the probability of label "1"; then label "0" has probability $1-p$ and

$$H(p) \;=\; -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p).$$

Notation warning: $H(p)$ here takes a single number $p \in [0,1]$ and means the entropy of the two-outcome distribution $(p, 1-p)$. Elsewhere $H(\cdot)$ takes a random variable. Both are standard; context disambiguates.

What to notice: the curve is symmetric about $p = 1/2$ (a 40/60 node is exactly as uncertain as a 60/40 node — swapping the label names cannot change how uncertain you are), it is concave (bulging upward — this single fact is what makes information gain non-negative, proved below), and it is flat near the top: moving from 50/50 to 40/60 costs only $0.029$ bits, while moving from 90/10 to 100/0 buys you $0.469$ bits. Purifying an already-lopsided node pays far better than nudging a balanced one.

The three facts to memorise, with their one-line reasons

FactWhy
$H(0) = H(1) = 0$Deterministic node (Property 2). Both terms vanish under the $0\log\frac10=0$ convention.
$H(1/2) = 1$ bit$-\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12 = \tfrac12(1)+\tfrac12(1) = 1$. The maximum, by Property 3 with $k=2$.

Proof — concavity of $H$

Work in nats ($\ln$) for cleaner derivatives; the base only multiplies by a positive constant, which cannot change concavity. With $H(p) = -p\ln p - (1-p)\ln(1-p)$:

$$H'(p) = -\ln p - 1 + \ln(1-p) + 1 = \ln\frac{1-p}{p},$$ $$H''(p) = \frac{d}{dp}\Big[\ln(1-p) - \ln p\Big] = \frac{-1}{1-p} - \frac{1}{p} = -\frac{1}{p(1-p)} \;\lt\; 0 \quad\text{for } p \in (0,1).$$

Strictly negative second derivative ⟹ strictly concave. Also $H'(1/2) = \ln 1 = 0$, confirming the maximum sits at $p = 1/2$. $\blacksquare$

Example — the running 40/60 node, by hand

The notes' example: $40\%$ of labels in $S$ are "1" and $60\%$ are "0", so $P(Y{=}1) = 0.4$, $P(Y{=}0) = 0.6$.

$$H(Y) = 0.4\log_2\frac{1}{0.4} + 0.6\log_2\frac{1}{0.6} = 0.4\log_2 2.5 + 0.6\log_2 1.\overline{6}.$$

Using $\log_2 2.5 = \log_2 5 - \log_2 2 = 2.321928 - 1 = 1.321928$ and $\log_2 \tfrac53 = \log_2 5 - \log_2 3 = 2.321928 - 1.584963 = 0.736966$:

$$H(Y) = 0.4(1.321928) + 0.6(0.736966) = 0.528771 + 0.442179 = \mathbf{0.970951} \text{ bits}.$$

Round to $0.971$. Worth committing to memory — a 40/60 split is the most common number on 07-280 problem sets, and it is only $0.029$ bits below the maximum of $1$.

Entropy of the labels inside a node

Everything above was about abstract distributions. Here is the translation into decision-tree language, which is exactly the move the notes make.

Let $S$ be the set of training examples sitting at some node, and let $\mathcal{Y}$ be the set of possible labels. Define the label distribution empirically — by counting:

$$P(Y = y) \;=\; \frac{\#\{\text{examples in } S \text{ with label } y\}}{|S|}.$$
Definition

The entropy of the labels at a node $S$ is

$$H(Y) \;=\; \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(Y=y)\,\log\frac{1}{P(Y=y)}.$$

Every symbol: $Y$ is the random variable "the label of an example drawn uniformly at random from $S$"; $y$ ranges over the possible labels ($\{0,1\}$ for us); $P(Y=y)$ is the fraction of $S$ carrying label $y$.

Example

The notes' instance: if $40\%$ of the labels in $S$ are "1" and $60\%$ are "0", then $P(Y{=}1) = 0.4$ and $P(Y{=}0) = 0.6$, so $H(Y) = 0.971$ bits (computed in full above).

Note that $H(Y)$ did not depend on $|S|$. A node with $4$ ones and $6$ zeros and a node with $400$ ones and $600$ zeros have identical entropy. Size enters later, through the weights in conditional entropy.

Common mistake

These probabilities are empirical frequencies computed from the node's own examples, not some prior belief and not the frequencies in the full dataset. When you recurse into a child, you recompute from scratch using only the examples that reached the child. Forgetting to recompute is the single most common arithmetic error on this material.

Specific conditional entropy and conditional entropy

We want to choose an attribute so that splitting on it reduces uncertainty as much as possible. So we need to answer: after the split, how uncertain are the labels?

Let $W$ be a random variable — later we will set $W$ to be the attribute we split on, so $W = x_b$ for some attribute index $b$, and $\omega$ ranges over its values ($0$ and $1$ for binary attributes).

Specific conditional entropy — the entropy of one child

Definition $$H(Y \mid W = \omega) \;=\; \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(Y = y \mid W = \omega)\,\log\frac{1}{P(Y = y \mid W = \omega)}.$$

The professor annotates this formula twice, and both annotations are the whole point:

  • "The conditioning $\mid W = \omega$ defines left or right child node." Fixing $\omega$ is literally the act of walking down one branch. $H(Y \mid x_b = 1)$ is "the entropy of the left child"; $H(Y \mid x_b = 0)$ is "the entropy of the right child". There is nothing probabilistic to worry about — just look at a subset of rows.
  • "$P(Y \mid W = \omega)$ is the distribution of labels when you take the subset of $S$ that has attribute $W = \omega$." So $P(Y{=}y \mid W{=}\omega) = \dfrac{\#\{\text{examples with } W=\omega \text{ and label } y\}}{\#\{\text{examples with } W=\omega\}}$ — count within the child, divide by the child's size.

It is called specific because it is pinned to one specific value $\omega$. It is a single number per child, not an average.

Intuition

Specific conditional entropy is nothing new — it is just $H$ applied to the child's label counts. If you can compute $H$ of a bag of labels, you can compute specific conditional entropy: take the bag, throw away every example whose attribute isn't $\omega$, recount, apply $H$. That's it.

Conditional entropy — the weighted average over children

Definition $$H(Y \mid W) \;=\; \sum_{\omega \in W} P(W = \omega)\cdot H(Y \mid W = \omega).$$

Here $P(W=\omega)$ is the fraction of $S$ that lands in the child $\omega$ — for a binary attribute, $\frac{|S_{\omega}|}{|S|}$. So for a binary split:

$$H(Y \mid W) = \frac{|S_1|}{|S|}H(Y \mid W{=}1) + \frac{|S_0|}{|S|}H(Y \mid W{=}0).$$

In the notes' words: "This is the average entropy across the child nodes (after the current node is split)."

Key idea — why the weighting matters enormously

Suppose a node has $100$ examples, $50$ of each label ($H(Y) = 1$ bit), and some attribute isolates a single example into the left child and dumps the other $99$ into the right child.

The left child is pure, so $H(Y \mid W{=}1) = 0$ — a perfect score! If we naively averaged the two children's entropies without weights we would get $\tfrac12(0) + \tfrac12(0.99993) = 0.49996$ bits and conclude this useless split halved our uncertainty.

With the correct weights: $\tfrac{1}{100}(0) + \tfrac{99}{100}(0.99993) = 0.98993$ bits. The split is credited with a gain of a mere $1 - 0.98993 = 0.01007$ bits, which is the truth: it resolved one example out of a hundred.

The weight $P(W=\omega)$ is what stops a split that isolates a single point from looking good. A pure child that contains almost nothing earns almost nothing.

Mutual information = information gain

Definition

The mutual information between the label $Y$ and the attribute $W$ is

$$I(Y; W) \;=\; H(Y) - H(Y \mid W).$$

In the notes' words: "Amount of reduction in entropy (before vs. after splitting)." In the decision-tree literature the same quantity is called the information gain of the split. Same number, two names.

Units: bits (with $\log_2$). Reading: "splitting on $W$ saves you $I(Y;W)$ yes/no questions, on average, when you later have to guess the label."

Analogy

You are playing 20 questions to guess a student's label. $H(Y)$ is what the game costs you cold. Then a friend whispers the value of attribute $W$ for free. $I(Y;W)$ is how many questions that whisper saved you, averaged over which whisper you get.

Where the analogy strains: the saving is an average over whispers, and a particular whisper can make the game harder — see the trap below. It is only the average that can never hurt.

Theorem: $I(Y;W) \ge 0$, i.e. $H(Y \mid W) \le H(Y)$

Proof (concavity of entropy / Jensen)

Step 1 — the parent's distribution is a mixture of the children's. By the law of total probability, for every label $y$,

$$P(Y = y) \;=\; \sum_{\omega} P(W=\omega)\, P(Y = y \mid W = \omega).$$

Reading this as vectors over $y$: the parent label distribution $P(Y)$ is the convex combination of the child label distributions $P(Y \mid W=\omega)$, with weights $P(W=\omega)$ (which are non-negative and sum to $1$). Concretely: pooling the children back together is the parent.

Step 2 — $H$ is concave as a function of the distribution. We showed $H'' \lt 0$ for the binary case; in general $H(p) = \sum_i p_i\log\frac1{p_i}$ is a sum of terms $-p_i \log p_i$, each concave in $p_i$ (second derivative $-1/(p_i \ln 2) \lt 0$), and a sum of concave functions is concave.

Step 3 — apply Jensen's inequality to the concave function $H$. For a concave $f$, $f(\sum_j \lambda_j z_j) \ge \sum_j \lambda_j f(z_j)$. With $f = H$, $\lambda_\omega = P(W=\omega)$ and $z_\omega = P(Y \mid W=\omega)$:

$$H(Y) = H\!\left(\sum_\omega P(W{=}\omega)\,P(Y\mid W{=}\omega)\right) \;\ge\; \sum_\omega P(W{=}\omega)\, H\big(P(Y \mid W{=}\omega)\big) \;=\; H(Y \mid W).$$

The left side is $H(Y)$ by Step 1; the right side is exactly the definition of conditional entropy. Therefore $I(Y;W) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid W) \ge 0$. $\blacksquare$

Equality condition. $H$ is strictly concave, so Jensen is tight only when all the $z_\omega$ with positive weight are identical: $P(Y \mid W = \omega) = P(Y)$ for every $\omega$. That is precisely the statement that $Y$ and $W$ are independent. So

$$H(Y \mid W) = H(Y) \iff I(Y;W) = 0 \iff Y \perp W.$$
Intuition

Why can't information hurt, on average? Because you are always free to ignore it. Any guessing strategy that doesn't look at $W$ is still available after you learn $W$, so the best strategy that does look at $W$ can only be at least as good. Averaged over which value of $W$ you see, uncertainty cannot go up.

In tree terms: splitting a node and then predicting the majority in each child is at least as accurate as predicting the majority in the parent, because "predict the parent's majority in both children" is one of the options available to you.

The exam trap: a specific conditional entropy CAN exceed $H(Y)$

Common mistake

The theorem says $H(Y \mid W) \le H(Y)$ — the weighted average over children never exceeds the parent. It does not say $H(Y \mid W = \omega) \le H(Y)$ for each individual $\omega$. That statement is false, and it is examined constantly.

A particular branch can leave you more confused than you started. Learning "the patient's test came back inconclusive" genuinely increases your uncertainty about the diagnosis; learning "the test was positive" decreases it a lot. On average you win, but not in every branch.

Symmetry: $I(Y;W) = I(W;Y)$

Proof, via the chain rule

Chain rule for entropy: $H(Y, W) = H(W) + H(Y \mid W)$. Derivation, using $P(y,w) = P(w)P(y\mid w)$:

$$H(Y,W) = -\sum_{y,w} P(y,w)\log P(y,w) = -\sum_{y,w} P(w)P(y\mid w)\big[\log P(w) + \log P(y\mid w)\big]$$ $$= -\sum_{w} P(w)\log P(w)\underbrace{\sum_y P(y\mid w)}_{=1} \;-\; \sum_w P(w)\underbrace{\Big[\sum_y P(y\mid w)\log P(y \mid w)\Big]}_{=\,-H(Y\mid W=w)} \;=\; H(W) + H(Y\mid W).$$

Now substitute into the definition of mutual information:

$$I(Y;W) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid W) = H(Y) - \big[H(Y,W) - H(W)\big] = H(Y) + H(W) - H(Y,W).$$

That expression is visibly symmetric in $Y$ and $W$ — swapping the two names leaves it unchanged. Hence $I(Y;W) = I(W;Y)$. $\blacksquare$

beyond syllabus The equivalent single-sum form makes symmetry even more obvious: $\displaystyle I(Y;W) = \sum_{y,w} P(y,w)\log\frac{P(y,w)}{P(y)P(w)}$, which is the KL divergence between the joint distribution and the product of the marginals — a direct measure of "how far from independent are they".

Intuition

Symmetry is a genuinely surprising fact. "How much does knowing the attribute tell me about the label" equals "how much does knowing the label tell me about the attribute". Information is a shared quantity, not a directional flow — which is why it is called mutual information.

BestAttribute, version 2: maximise mutual information

We can now fill in the missing subroutine from Chapter 6's DT algorithm:

function BestAttribute(S, A):
    H_Y <- entropy of the labels in S                  # one number, fixed
    best <- none; bestI <- -infinity
    for each attribute b in A:
        S1 <- {(x,y) in S : x_b = 1}
        S0 <- {(x,y) in S : x_b = 0}
        H_cond <- (|S1|/|S|) * entropy(labels of S1)
                + (|S0|/|S|) * entropy(labels of S0)
        I <- H_Y - H_cond                              # information gain
        if I > bestI: bestI <- I; best <- b
    return best

The notes state the rule as: "BestAttribute chooses the attribute that maximizes mutual information (equivalently, minimizes conditional entropy)." Both forms:

$$b^\star \;=\; \argmax_{b \in A} \; I(Y; x_b) \;=\; \argmax_{b \in A} \big[H(Y) - H(Y \mid x_b)\big] \;=\; \argmin_{b \in A} \; H(Y \mid x_b).$$
Why the two are equivalent

$H(Y)$ is computed from the labels in $S$ alone. It does not mention $b$ at all — it is the same constant for every candidate attribute at this node. Subtracting a constant from a collection of numbers reverses nothing and shifts everything equally, so

$$\argmax_b \big[\underbrace{H(Y)}_{\text{const}} - H(Y\mid x_b)\big] = \argmax_b \big[-H(Y\mid x_b)\big] = \argmin_b H(Y\mid x_b). \quad\blacksquare$$

Practical consequence: at a given node you may skip computing $H(Y)$ entirely and just rank the conditional entropies, smallest wins. Compute $H(Y)$ only when you want to report the gain (or when comparing across nodes, where $H(Y)$ does change).

Common mistake

$H(Y)$ is constant within one node, not across the tree. Each recursive call recomputes it from that node's examples. Gains at different nodes are not directly comparable — a gain of $0.3$ bits in a node holding $4$ examples is worth far less than $0.3$ bits in a node holding $400$.

Step 5 — contrast with the training-error criterion

Under training error (Chapter 6's criterion), every split's score is mistakes made by majority vote in each child:

  • $x_1$: the $x_1{=}1$ child has $3$ zeros and $4$ ones → vote $1$ → $3$ mistakes; the $x_1{=}0$ child is pure → $0$. Total $3/8$.
  • $x_2$: $x_2{=}1$ is pure → $0$; $x_2{=}0$ has $3$ zeros, $1$ one → vote $0$ → $1$ mistake. Total $1/8$.
  • $x_3$: $x_3{=}1$ has $1$ zero, $3$ ones → vote $1$ → $1$ mistake; $x_3{=}0$ is a $2$–$2$ tie → vote either → $2$ mistakes. Total $3/8$.
  • No split at all: vote $1$ → $3$ mistakes. $3/8$.
Common mistake — and the reason the course switches criteria

Training error does get the winner right here ($x_2$, $1/8$). But look at what it says about the other two: $x_1$, $x_3$ and doing nothing at all all score $3/8$. A three-way dead tie, including a tie with "don't split".

Mutual information separates them cleanly: $I(Y;x_1) = 0.0924$ versus $I(Y;x_3) = 0.0488$ — $x_1$ is nearly twice as informative as $x_3$, and both are strictly better than not splitting ($I = 0$ would mean useless). Error rate cannot express any of that, because in each case the majority label simply did not flip.

On a dataset where the best attribute happens to be one of the tied ones, error rate picks arbitrarily and the greedy algorithm goes down the wrong path. That is the failure Chapter 6 demonstrated, and this is the repair.

At the root above, suppose someone proposes a fourth attribute $x_4$ that happens to equal $y$ for every training example. What are $H(Y \mid x_4)$ and $I(Y;x_4)$?
Why: both children would be perfectly pure ($x_4{=}1$ holds only label-1 examples, $x_4{=}0$ only label-0 ones), so each specific conditional entropy is $0$ and so is their weighted average. Then $I = H(Y) - 0 = H(Y) = 0.954434$ — and that is the ceiling: since $H(Y\mid W) \ge 0$ always, gain can never exceed $H(Y)$. Option C is impossible because we proved $I \ge 0$. Option A describes the opposite extreme, independence.

ID3, C4.5, and the bias toward many-valued attributes

beyond syllabus The algorithm you just built — recursive splitting with BestAttribute = argmax information gain — is ID3 (Quinlan, 1986). Its successor C4.5 adds real-valued thresholds, missing-value handling, pruning, and one fix that matters here.

The bug. Information gain systematically prefers attributes with many possible values. Take our 8-row dataset and add an attribute $x_{\text{id}} \in \{1,\dots,8\}$ that is just the row number. Splitting on it gives eight children of one example each, every one pure, so

$$H(Y \mid x_{\text{id}}) = \sum_{\omega=1}^{8}\tfrac18 \cdot 0 = 0, \qquad I(Y; x_{\text{id}}) = 0.954434 - 0 = 0.954434,$$

the maximum possible gain — beating $x_2$'s $0.548795$. Yet the resulting "tree" is a lookup table that memorises the training set and generalises to nothing.

The fix: gain ratio. Penalise by the entropy of the attribute itself (called the split information):

$$\text{GainRatio}(Y;W) = \frac{I(Y;W)}{H(W)}, \qquad H(W) = \sum_\omega P(W{=}\omega)\log\frac{1}{P(W{=}\omega)}.$$
  • $x_{\text{id}}$: $H(x_{\text{id}}) = \log_2 8 = 3$ bits, so GainRatio $= 0.954434 / 3 = \mathbf{0.318145}$.
  • $x_2$: it splits $4$–$4$, so $H(x_2) = 1$ bit, and GainRatio $= 0.548795 / 1 = \mathbf{0.548795}$.

$x_2$ now wins comfortably. The intuition: an attribute that shatters the data into many tiny pieces has to pay for the shattering, and the price is its own entropy.

CART instead uses Gini impurity $\sum_y p_y(1-p_y)$, which the notes mention and explicitly do not discuss. It behaves almost identically to entropy — both are concave, both are $0$ exactly at purity, both are maximised at uniform — and the two criteria disagree on roughly $2\%$ of splits in practice. Gini is slightly cheaper because it needs no logarithm.

Guided problemA child got worse — is the split bad?

A node holds $12$ examples: $10$ labelled "0" and $2$ labelled "1". Attribute $W$ splits it into

  • $W{=}1$: $4$ examples — $2$ zeros and $2$ ones;
  • $W{=}0$: $8$ examples — $8$ zeros and $0$ ones.

Your lab partner says: "Throw this split away. The left child has entropy $1.0$, which is higher than the parent's. The split increased uncertainty, and we proved that's impossible, so I must have made an arithmetic error." Is your partner right? Compute everything.

Step 1 — the parent's entropy

$P(Y{=}0) = 10/12 = 0.833333$, $P(Y{=}1) = 2/12 = 0.166667$.

$$\log_2\tfrac{12}{10} = \log_2 12 - \log_2 10 = 3.584963 - 3.321928 = 0.263034,$$ $$\log_2 6 = 2.584963,$$ $$H(Y) = 0.833333(0.263034) + 0.166667(2.584963) = 0.219195 + 0.430827 = \mathbf{0.650022}\text{ bits}.$$
Step 2 — the two specific conditional entropies

$W{=}1$: two of each label, so $P(Y{=}1\mid W{=}1) = 1/2$ and

$$H(Y \mid W{=}1) = \mathbf{1.000000}\text{ bit}.$$

$W{=}0$: all eight are zeros, so the node is pure and

$$H(Y \mid W{=}0) = \mathbf{0.000000}.$$

Your partner's observation is factually correct: $1.000000 \gt 0.650022$. The left child really is more uncertain than the parent.

Step 3 — the theorem says nothing about individual children

The theorem is $H(Y\mid W) \le H(Y)$, where $H(Y\mid W)$ is the weighted average $\sum_\omega P(W{=}\omega)H(Y\mid W{=}\omega)$. It is not a claim about each $H(Y\mid W{=}\omega)$ separately, and indeed that claim is false — this problem is the counterexample.

Why it can happen: Jensen's inequality concerns the value of a concave function at a mixture of points versus the mixture of the values. Nothing stops one of the individual points from sitting higher than the mixture point; what's guaranteed is that the average of the values sits below.

Weights: $P(W{=}1) = 4/12 = 1/3$ and $P(W{=}0) = 8/12 = 2/3$.

Answer & the takeaway
$$H(Y\mid W) = \tfrac13(1.000000) + \tfrac23(0.000000) = \mathbf{0.333333}\text{ bits}.$$ $$I(Y;W) = 0.650022 - 0.333333 = \mathbf{0.316689}\text{ bits} \;\gt\; 0. \quad\checkmark$$

Your partner is wrong, and there is no arithmetic error. The split is a good one: it cut uncertainty roughly in half. Two thirds of the data landed in a perfectly pure child that never needs another question; the remaining third became a coin flip, but it is only a third.

Takeaway — memorise the two statements separately:

  • $H(Y\mid W) \le H(Y)$  — always true (the weighted average).
  • $H(Y\mid W{=}\omega) \le H(Y)$  — often false (a single branch).

Confusing the two is the most reliably examined error on this material. Set the calculator above to left $= (2, 2)$ and right $= (8, 0)$ and watch it happen.

Guided problemWhen maximum gain is the wrong answer

A dataset of $N = 100$ patients, label $y = $ "has the condition", with $50$ yes and $50$ no. Two candidate attributes:

  • $W_A$ = "blood test positive", binary. Splits $50/50$; the positive child is $45$ yes and $5$ no, the negative child is $5$ yes and $45$ no.
  • $W_B$ = "patient ID", taking $100$ distinct values, one per patient.

Compute $I(Y;W_A)$ and $I(Y;W_B)$. Which does information gain pick? Which should you pick, and what fixes the criterion?

Step 1 — the parent, and $W_A$

$H(Y) = H(1/2) = \mathbf{1.000000}$ bit (balanced).

Each $W_A$ child is $45$–$5$, i.e. $p = 0.1$:

$$H(Y\mid W_A{=}\text{pos}) = H(Y\mid W_A{=}\text{neg}) = 0.9\log_2\tfrac{10}{9} + 0.1\log_2 10 = 0.9(0.152003)+0.1(3.321928)$$ $$= 0.136803 + 0.332193 = \mathbf{0.468996}.$$ $$H(Y\mid W_A) = \tfrac12(0.468996) + \tfrac12(0.468996) = \mathbf{0.468996}, \qquad I(Y;W_A) = 1 - 0.468996 = \mathbf{0.531004}.$$
Step 2 — $W_B$, the ID attribute

Each of the $100$ values selects exactly one patient. A one-example node is pure, so every specific conditional entropy is $0$:

$$H(Y\mid W_B) = \sum_{\omega=1}^{100}\tfrac{1}{100}\cdot 0 = \mathbf{0.000000}, \qquad I(Y;W_B) = 1 - 0 = \mathbf{1.000000}\text{ bit}.$$

The absolute maximum — you cannot gain more than $H(Y)$, and $W_B$ takes all of it. Information gain picks $W_B$.

Step 3 — why that is catastrophic

The resulting tree has $100$ leaves, one per training patient. Training error: $0$. Test error: for a new patient with an unseen ID, the tree has no branch at all — and if you force one, the prediction is a coin flip. It has memorised, not learned.

The mechanism of the failure: gain measures reduction in entropy on the training set, and any attribute fine enough to separate every example drives that to zero regardless of whether the attribute means anything. This is overfitting showing up inside the splitting criterion itself.

Answer & the takeaway

$I(Y;W_A) = 0.531004$, $I(Y;W_B) = 1.000000$. Raw information gain picks $W_B$. Gain ratio repairs it:

$$H(W_A) = H(1/2) = 1 \Rightarrow \text{GainRatio}(W_A) = \frac{0.531004}{1} = \mathbf{0.531004},$$ $$H(W_B) = \log_2 100 = 6.643856 \Rightarrow \text{GainRatio}(W_B) = \frac{1.000000}{6.643856} = \mathbf{0.150515}.$$

$W_A$ now wins by more than a factor of three. beyond syllabus

Takeaway: information gain is the right local criterion but it has no notion of generalisation. In this course we split on binary attributes, where every attribute has $H(W) \le 1$ and the bias is invisible. The moment you allow many-valued or real-valued attributes, you need gain ratio (C4.5), a depth limit, or pruning.

A node has $100$ examples, $50$ of each label. Split A produces children of sizes $50$ and $50$, each $40$–$10$ and $10$–$40$. Split B produces children of sizes $2$ and $98$, where the size-2 child is pure. Without computing exactly, which split has the larger information gain, and why?
Why: conditional entropy is a weighted average. For split A, both children are $p = 0.2$, so $H(Y\mid W) = 0.5(0.721928) + 0.5(0.721928) = 0.721928$ and the gain is $1 - 0.721928 = 0.278$ bits. For split B, the pure child carries weight $2/100 = 0.02$ and contributes $0$, while the $98$-example child is still nearly balanced ($48$–$50$, $H \approx 0.9997$), contributing $0.98(0.9997) = 0.9797$; the gain is about $0.020$ bits. Split A wins by more than a factor of thirteen. The weighting is the entire reason — it is what stops "isolate one point" from scoring well.
