Part I · Chapter 3

Adversarial search: Minimax, α-β pruning & Expectimax

By the end of this page you will be able to take any small game tree, compute its minimax, α-β and expectimax values by hand, name the action the agent should play, say exactly which branches get pruned and why, and explain what breaks when your model of the opponent is wrong.

Why games, and what changes from Chapter 1

In search and informed search the world sat still while you thought. You had a start state, a transition model, a goal test, and the universe politely waited for you to plan a route. The answer was a sequence: Left, Left, Up, Right.

Now something in the world pushes back. In a game there is another agent choosing actions, and that agent gets to choose after seeing what you did. A plan that is a fixed sequence of moves is worthless: your second move has to depend on what your opponent just played. This one change — a second decision-maker interleaved with yours — is what "adversarial" means, and it forces a different kind of answer out of the algorithm.

Key idea

Single-agent search asks "what path gets me to the goal?" and returns a sequence of actions. Adversarial search asks "what should I do in every situation the opponent can put me in?" and returns a strategy — a rule that maps any reachable state to a move. The tree looks similar. The output type is completely different.

Where the uncertainty comes from

An opponent is only one source of uncertainty, and the lecture opens by listing the others, because they all end up handled by the same machinery:

  • Hidden information — the cards in another player's hand. You genuinely cannot see part of the state.
  • Noise — sensor noise on a robot. You see the state, but through a distorting lens.
  • Too complicated to model — leaves blowing in the wind. It is deterministic physics in principle; it is randomness in practice.
  • Infinitely many possible configurations — the continuous world doesn't come pre-discretised.
  • More possibilities than any computer can enumerate in reasonable time — tic-tac-toe → checkers → chess. Nothing is hidden in chess; it is just too big.

That last bullet is worth staring at. In chess there is no hidden information, no dice, no noise. The entire game is visible on the board. And yet no machine and no human will ever play it perfectly. Computational limits produce uncertainty that is, from the agent's point of view, indistinguishable from randomness. Half of this chapter (evaluation functions, depth limits, pruning) exists only because of that.

Seventy-five years of game AI, and what it actually taught us

The history here is not trivia. Each milestone is a lesson about which idea mattered, and every one of those ideas shows up later in this course or the next one.

Checkers

The first computer checkers player appeared in 1950. In 1994, Chinook became the first computer world champion in any game, ending Marion Tinsley's forty-year reign as human champion — a reign in which he lost a handful of games total, to anyone. Chinook leaned on a complete 8-piece endgame database: every position with eight or fewer pieces on the board had its true value looked up, not searched. In 2007 checkers was solved outright, with an endgame database of 39 trillion states; the game is a draw with perfect play on both sides.

Lesson: search from the front, lookup from the back, meet in the middle. And Samuel's checkers work is where machine learning of an evaluation function — a direct ancestor of reinforcement learning — was born.

Chess

The founding generation, roughly 1945–1960, is a who's-who: Zuse, Wiener, Shannon, Turing, Newell, Simon, McCarthy. Shannon's 1950 paper laid out the whole recipe still in use: game tree, depth limit, evaluation function. Then came decades of steady grinding on that "standard model."

In 1997 Deep Blue beat Garry Kasparov in a six-game match, examining 200 million positions per second and extending its search up to 40 ply along forcing lines. ("Ply" = one player's move; two plies make one full round of chess.) Today a program on an ordinary PC rates above 3200 Elo, against 2870 for Magnus Carlsen. The gap is not close and will not close.

Lesson: brute force plus a good evaluation function plus α-β pruning was enough to pass humans, decades before anything that looked like modern learning. Iterative deepening as a practical technique comes out of this era of chess engineering.

Go

Zobrist's 1968 program could play legal Go moves — and that was the achievement. The branching factor is b > 300, so the tree explodes far faster than chess, and nobody could write an evaluation function for a Go position that was worth anything: a stone's value depends on a whole-board fight forty moves away. From 2005–2014, Monte Carlo tree search changed that: instead of evaluating a position with hand-built features, play thousands of fast random games from it and use the win rate. Programs jumped from hopeless to beating strong amateurs, then professionals at a handicap. AlphaGo's 2016 win over Lee Sedol bolted a learned neural evaluation onto exactly that MCTS skeleton. beyond syllabus

Intuition

What changed across all three games was never "we found the true minimax value." It was always one of: (1) search deeper for the same money — α-β pruning; (2) judge a non-final position better — evaluation functions; (3) replace a hand-written judgement with a learned or sampled one — Samuel, MCTS, AlphaGo. Those three levers are the whole chapter.

Zero-sum versus general games

Definition

A game is zero-sum if the players' utilities are exactly opposed: for every terminal state $s$, $\text{Utility}(s,\text{P}_1) + \text{Utility}(s,\text{P}_2) = 0$. (Any constant sum works identically — "zero-sum" is the conventional name.) A general game puts no such constraint on the utilities; each agent simply has its own.

Why does anyone care about this technicality? Because of a bookkeeping miracle. In a zero-sum game, player two's utility carries no information that player one's utility doesn't already carry: it is just the negative. So instead of labelling every node with a vector of utilities, you can label every node with a single number — player one's utility — and describe the two players' behaviour with two words:

  • Player one (call them MAX) wants that number as large as possible.
  • Player two (MIN) wants their own utility large, which is the same as wanting that number as small as possible.

One number, two opposite preferences. That is the entire reason the algorithm is called mini-max and not something with a subscript per player.

General games are qualitatively different, not just messier. With independent utilities you can get cooperation, indifference, competition, shifting alliances, and mixtures of these — two players can find a move that helps them both at a third player's expense without ever communicating. We handle that case in the generalized minimax section; the miracle above stops applying and the values become tuples again.

The standard game formulation

The course focuses on "standard" games: deterministic, fully observable, two-player, turn-taking, zero-sum. That is one specific corner of the five-axis space, and the rest of this chapter is a tour of what you do when you fall out of that corner.

Definition

A standard game is the tuple:

  • $s_0$ — the initial state, the starting configuration of the board.
  • $\text{Player}(s)$ — whose move it is in state $s$. In chess, whether it is white's or black's turn.
  • $\text{Actions}(s)$ — the set of legal moves available to $\text{Player}(s)$ at $s$.
  • $\text{Result}(s,a)$ — the transition model: the state you land in after playing $a$ in $s$.
  • $\text{Terminal-Test}(s)$ — true iff the game is over at $s$ (checkmate, stalemate, board full).
  • $\text{Utility}(s,p)$ — the payoff to player $p$ at terminal state $s$. In a zero-sum two-player game you can drop the $p$ and write $\text{Utility}(s)$, always meaning "the value to the player deciding at the root."

Compare this line by line with the search problem from Chapter 1. $s_0$, $\text{Actions}$, $\text{Result}$ — identical. The two changes are: $\text{Player}(s)$ is new (someone else moves on alternate turns), and the goal test has been replaced by a terminal test plus a number. In search, a state was goal or not-goal: a bit. In a game, terminating isn't the point — terminating well is. So we carry a real-valued utility instead of a boolean.

The output is a policy, not a plan. Belabour this.

Here is the sentence from the notes: "Our goal is to calculate a contingent plan, also called a strategy or policy, which recommends a move for every possible game state." Three words for the same object — contingent plan = strategy = policy — and the important word is contingent.

In Chapter 1 the solution to the 8-puzzle was a list: [Up, Left, Left, Down]. You could write it on a napkin, hand it to someone, and they'd solve the puzzle by reading it out. Nothing could interfere.

Try that in chess. You write down [e4, Nf3, Bc4, Qh5, Qxf7#] and hand it over. Your opponent plays something other than the moves your napkin silently assumed, and by move two the list is nonsense — it may not even be legal. The napkin failed not because the moves were bad but because it was the wrong type of object.

Analogy

A sequence of actions is a recipe: step 1, step 2, step 3, do them in order. A policy is a flowchart, or better, a chess opening book: "after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3, if they play Nc6 then Bb5; if they play Nf6 then Nxe5; if they play d6 then d4…" It has an answer prepared for every branch, including branches that will never happen. That is why it is called contingent — it is a plan contingent on what the other player does.

Where the analogy strains: a real opening book only covers sensible moves and shrugs at nonsense. A true policy is total — defined at every reachable state, including the absurd ones.

Formally: a policy is a function $\pi : S \to A$ with $\pi(s) \in \text{Actions}(s)$ for every state $s$ where it is your turn. Its size is on the order of the number of reachable states, not the length of a path. That is why a game solution is exponentially bigger than a search solution, and why the algorithms below spend all their effort on not writing the whole thing down. Minimax computes the policy implicitly: it hands you the value of every child of wherever you are now, and you take the argmax; do that again each turn and you have executed the policy without ever storing it. You will meet the symbol $\pi$ again, with exactly this meaning, when this course reaches MDPs and reinforcement learning.

Your opponent makes a move you thought was terrible and never planned for. What does a correct policy do?
Why: "Contingent" means total. Minimax assumes optimal play when it computes values, but the object it defines is still a move for every state — if the opponent blunders, you are in a state whose value you can compute and whose best action you can take, and it will be at least as good as the value you were guaranteed. The failure mode of a wrong opponent model is losing value (see modelling assumptions), never having no move at all.

Minimax: the value of a state under optimal play

Definition

The minimax value of a state is the utility (to MAX) of that state assuming both players play optimally from there on. Equivalently: the best outcome the player to move can guarantee, no matter what the opponent does.

$$ V(s) \;=\; \begin{cases} \text{Utility}(s) & \text{if } \text{Terminal-Test}(s)\\[4pt] \max_{a \in \text{Actions}(s)} V(\text{Result}(s,a)) & \text{if } \text{Player}(s) = \text{MAX}\\[4pt] \min_{a \in \text{Actions}(s)} V(\text{Result}(s,a)) & \text{if } \text{Player}(s) = \text{MIN} \end{cases} $$

Read the symbols: $V(s)$ is a single number, the value of state $s$ to MAX. $\text{Result}(s,a)$ is the child state you reach by playing $a$. The $\max$ and $\min$ range over MAX's and MIN's legal actions at $s$. Nothing here is approximate — this is a definition by recursion on the game tree, bottoming out at terminal states.

Note the word guarantee. It is the strongest claim in this chapter. If minimax says a state is worth $+4$ to you, then there exists a strategy for you that gets at least $4$ against every possible opponent, optimal or not. And if your opponent plays optimally, you cannot get more than 4. It is a floor you can always reach and a ceiling a good opponent can always impose.

Drawing the tree

We draw states as nodes and actions as branches. Conventionally (and in your lecture slides):

  • MAX nodes — blue, upward-pointing triangles. The point of the triangle points up: "push the value up." The node's value is the maximum over its children.
  • MIN nodes — red, downward-pointing triangles. The node's value is the minimum over its children.
  • Terminal states — squares, holding the number $\text{Utility}(s)$ directly.
Common mistake

Every number in the tree is written from MAX's point of view. A leaf labelled $-2$ does not mean "MIN scores 2." It means MAX scores $-2$, and therefore (zero-sum) MIN scores $+2$. MIN's value of any node is just the negation of the printed number. Students who start silently flipping signs at MIN nodes get every problem wrong; MIN minimises the printed number, and that is already MIN acting in its own self-interest.

The worked example from the notes

The blue MAX player moves first and chooses Left, Center, or Right. Then the red MIN player picks one of its two replies, trying to make the resulting number as small as possible. The six leaves are $3, 12 \mid 4, 6 \mid 15, 2$.

Working from the leaves upward:

  • Left MIN node: $\min(3, 12) = 3$.
  • Center MIN node: $\min(4, 6) = 4$.
  • Right MIN node: $\min(15, 2) = 2$.
  • Root MAX node: $\max(3, 4, 2) = 4$.
Key idea

The minimax value at the root is $4$ — but the number 4 is not the answer. The agent cannot "play a 4." It must select an action. The answer is Center, the action whose child carries the value 4.

$$\hat{a} \;=\; \argmax_{a \in \text{Actions}(s_0)} V(\text{Result}(s_0,a)), \qquad V(s_0) = \max_{a} V(\text{Result}(s_0,a))$$

The value $V$ and the decision $\hat a$ are computed by the same sweep; one is the max, the other is the argmax. Exams ask for the action. Give the action.

Notice what "Right looks great" costs you. The Right branch contains 15, the largest leaf in the tree. A greedy player who scans for big numbers picks Right and receives 2 — the worst of the three options — because MIN gets to answer and will obviously choose 2 over 15. Minimax is immune to this because it evaluates each of your moves by what the opponent will do afterwards, not by the best thing that could conceivably happen.

The generic pseudocode

Straight from the notes. Two functions: one that picks the move at the root, one recursive routine that computes a value.

function max_decision(state):
    return the action in state.actions that gives
           the max value( state.result(action) )

function value(state):
    if state.is_leaf:
        return state.value
    if state.player is MAX:
        return max over value(state.result(a)) for a in state.actions
    if state.player is MIN:
        return min over value(state.result(a)) for a in state.actions

Three things to note. (1) There is exactly one recursive function, dispatching on whose turn it is — you do not need separate max_value/min_value routines, though people often write them for clarity (and α-β will make us write them). (2) The base case is is_leaf, i.e. Terminal-Test. (3) max_decision returns an action, value returns a number: the type distinction from the Key idea box, made concrete in code.

The same skeleton with a chance case bolted on — expectiminimax — is what we'll use in the expectimax section:

function value(state):
    if state.is_leaf:
        return state.value
    if state.player is MAX:
        return max over value(state.result(a)) for a in state.actions
    if state.player is MIN:
        return min over value(state.result(a)) for a in state.actions
    if state.player is CHANCE:
        return sum over P(s) * value(s) for s in state.next_states

One extra branch. That is the whole difference between minimax and expectiminimax.

Guided problem 1Compute the minimax value and name the action

A MAX root has three actions A, B, C. Each leads to a MIN node. The MIN nodes' children are terminal:

Root actionMIN node's terminal children
A−1, 8, 4
B7, 3
C6, 6, 20

(a) What is the minimax value at the root? (b) Which action should MAX play? (c) MAX plays your answer and MIN, being careless, replies with the move that leads to the largest leaf in that subtree instead. What does MAX actually score, and does that contradict anything?

Step 1 — What kind of node is each, and in what order do I evaluate?

Leaves have their values given. The three interior nodes are MIN nodes — the opponent moves there — so each takes the minimum of its children. The root is a MAX node and takes the maximum of the three MIN values. Always evaluate bottom-up: you cannot know the root until you know its children.

Step 2 — Evaluate the three MIN nodes

$V(A) = \min(-1, 8, 4) = -1$. The 8 and the 4 are irrelevant; MIN will play the $-1$.

$V(B) = \min(7, 3) = 3$.

$V(C) = \min(6, 6, 20) = 6$. The 20 is a trap for the greedy eye — MIN will never allow it.

Step 3 — Evaluate the root

$V(\text{root}) = \max(-1,\; 3,\; 6) = 6$, achieved by the child reached through action C.

Answer & the takeaway

(a) The minimax value is $\mathbf{6}$. (b) MAX plays $\mathbf{C}$. Not "6" — C.

(c) If MIN carelessly plays toward the 20, MAX scores 20. This contradicts nothing. Minimax guarantees at least the minimax value; it is a lower bound on what you get, and a suboptimal opponent can only hand you more. The guarantee is one-sided.

Takeaway: notice that the tree contains the numbers 8 and 20, both far larger than 6, and neither is reachable against a competent opponent. The size of the numbers in a subtree tells you nothing. Only the value that survives the alternating min/max filter matters.

What minimax costs, and bounded rationality

Minimax as written is just exhaustive depth-first search of the game tree, with an arithmetic operation on the way back up. So its complexity is DFS's complexity.

Proof

Time: $O(b^m)$. Let $b$ be the branching factor (legal moves per state) and $m$ the maximum depth of the tree in plies. The root generates $b$ children; each of those generates $b$, giving $b^2$ at depth 2; by induction depth $k$ holds $b^k$ nodes. Every node must be visited, because a node's value depends on all of its children (the max or min is over the complete set). Total work:

$$1 + b + b^2 + \cdots + b^m \;=\; \frac{b^{m+1}-1}{b-1} \;=\; \Theta(b^m)$$

The sum is dominated by its last term — the bottom level alone contains more nodes than all the levels above it combined (for $b \ge 2$). So the exponent is what matters and constants are irrelevant.

Space: $O(bm)$. DFS holds only the current root-to-leaf path in memory, which is $m$ nodes deep. At each node on that path we must remember its generated-but-not-yet-explored siblings so we can continue the loop after the recursive call returns — that is at most $b$ children per level. Hence $b$ items on each of $m$ levels: $O(bm)$. Note it is $b \times m$, not $b^m$: memory is polynomial while time is exponential. That asymmetry is exactly why we use DFS here instead of BFS.

Plug in chess

For chess, $b \approx 35$ and $m \approx 100$. So the tree has on the order of

$$35^{100} \approx 10^{154}$$

nodes. For scale: there are roughly $10^{80}$ atoms in the observable universe. You could devote every atom to storing a node, run since the Big Bang, and not finish a rounding error's worth of the tree. Exact solution of chess is not "hard", it is physically impossible, and always will be. The lecture's summary slide notes video games are worse still: $b = 10^{500}$, $|S| = 10^{4000}$, $m = 10{,}000$.

Intuition

And yet humans play chess well. Humans do not have a secret polynomial algorithm; a grandmaster examines perhaps a hundred positions before a move, seven orders of magnitude fewer than Deep Blue. What they have is a very good approximate evaluation of a position and a very good sense of which few moves are worth looking at.

Definition

Bounded rationality (Herbert Simon, 1957) — the observation that real decision-makers, human or machine, do not optimise. They are limited in the information they have, in the time available, and in their own ability to compute. So rationality must be redefined relative to those limits: a bounded-rational agent satisfices, searching until it finds an option good enough rather than provably best.

This is a genuinely important idea and not just an excuse for approximation. The classical definition — "a rational agent maximises expected utility" — is vacuous when the maximisation cannot be performed. Simon's move is to treat the cost of deliberating as part of the problem: the right question is not "what is the optimal move?" but "what is the best move I can identify with the computation I can afford?" It won him a Nobel Prize in economics, and every technique in the remainder of this chapter — depth limits, evaluation functions, pruning, Monte Carlo sampling — is an answer to that second question. Chess engines are not failed exact solvers; they are successful bounded-rational agents.

Resource limits: bounded lookahead and the horizon

The problem: in a realistic game you cannot search to the leaves. The recursion in value() bottoms out at is_leaf, and it never gets there before your clock runs out.

Solution 1: bounded lookahead. Two changes to the algorithm:

  1. Search only to a preset depth limit, also called the horizon.
  2. When you hit that depth on a non-terminal position, call an evaluation function $\text{EVAL}(s)$ and pretend the number it returns is the utility.

The change to the code is two lines: pass a depth counter down, and replace if state.is_leaf: return state.value with if state.is_leaf or depth == 0: return EVAL(state).

Common mistake

The guarantee of optimal play is gone. Depth-limited minimax no longer computes the minimax value; it computes the minimax value of a different, truncated game whose payoffs are your evaluation function's guesses. Everything proved above about guaranteed outcomes evaporates. This is not a small caveat — it means a depth-limited engine can be talked into a bad position by anything that hides the consequences just past its horizon.

What to notice: the numbers −1, −2, 4, 9 are not utilities of finished games. They are EVAL's opinions about unfinished positions. The MIN row takes min(−1,−2) = −2 and min(4,9) = 4; the root takes max(−2,4) = 4 and plays right. If EVAL is wrong about any of those four positions, the root's decision can be wrong, and nothing in the algorithm will notice.

The arithmetic that sets the depth

The slides work a concrete budget, and you should be able to redo it for any numbers on an exam:

  • Suppose you have 100 seconds per move and your engine explores 10,000 nodes/second.
  • Then you can afford $100 \times 10{,}000 = \mathbf{10^6}$ nodes per move.
  • Plain minimax visits about $b^d$ nodes to depth $d$. With $b \approx 35$: $35^4 = 1{,}500{,}625 \approx 10^6$. So $d \approx 4$.

Depth 4 means "I move, you move, I move, you move" — two moves each. The slides' verdict: "not so good." A club player looks further than that. Solving $35^d = 10^6$ exactly gives $d = \log_{35}(10^6) = 6/\log_{10}35 \approx 6/1.544 \approx 3.9$. Hold on to this number: α-β pruning will double it, and that one improvement is the difference between an engine that embarrasses itself and one that does not.

Depth matters

Three claims from the slides, in order of importance:

  1. Evaluation functions are always imperfect. If yours were perfect it would return the true minimax value, you would search depth 1, and the game would be solved. It isn't and you can't.
  2. Deeper search ⟹ better play (usually). Why? Because errors made by EVAL at the frontier get filtered on the way up through many layers of max and min. A position that merely looks good at depth 2 has had 6 more plies of the opponent's best objections applied to it by depth 8. Deeper search converts a noisy heuristic into a sharper decision. The hedge "usually" is real, though: pathological trees exist where deeper minimax is worse, because minimax propagates the extreme of a set of noisy estimates and extremes are biased. beyond syllabus
  3. Or: deeper search buys the same quality of play from a less accurate evaluation function. This is the framing that makes it an engineering decision rather than a fact.
Key idea

This is the tradeoff between complexity of features and complexity of computation. You have a fixed budget of nodes per move. A richer evaluation function (more features, slower per call) costs you nodes and therefore depth. A crude, fast one buys depth. Neither extreme wins: an EVAL that just counts material is fast but blind; an EVAL that is nearly the true minimax value is so slow you only see one ply. Real engines sit in the middle and tune the balance empirically. You will meet exactly this tradeoff again as the bias–variance and model-capacity story in Part II.

Evaluation functions

Evaluation functions score non-terminals in depth-limited search. They answer: "this game isn't over — how good does it look for MAX?"

The ideal evaluation function returns the actual minimax value of the position. Then bounded lookahead is exact. Of course, computing it is the problem we were trying to avoid, so the ideal is a definition of the target, not a proposal.

In practice, the standard form is a weighted linear sum of features:

$$\text{EVAL}(s) \;=\; w_1 f_1(s) + w_2 f_2(s) + \cdots + w_n f_n(s)$$

Naming every symbol: $f_i(s)$ is the $i$-th feature, a number you can compute cheaply from the board — not a probability, just a measurement. $w_i$ is the weight saying how much that feature matters and in which direction. $\text{EVAL}(s)$ is the resulting score, positive for "good for MAX."

The classic chess example from the slides: $w_1 = 9$ with $f_1(s) = (\text{number of white queens} - \text{number of black queens})$. Read it carefully — the feature is a difference, so it is automatically zero-sum-symmetric and automatically negative when black is ahead. The weight 9 is the queen's value in pawn units. A full material term is $9\Delta Q + 5\Delta R + 3\Delta B + 3\Delta N + 1\Delta P$, plus positional features: pawn structure, king safety, centre control, mobility (number of legal moves), rook on an open file.

Example

Evaluation for Pacman. A depth-limited Pacman agent that only scores "current game score" plays terribly — it stalls, because eating a dot two steps away and eating it five steps away look identical inside the horizon. Useful features:

  • $f_1$ = current game score (weight strongly positive)
  • $f_2$ = negative distance to the nearest food — this is where the heuristics of the last chapter come back as features
  • $f_3$ = number of remaining food pellets (weight negative: fewer left is better)
  • $f_4$ = distance to the nearest non-scared ghost (positive, and usually capped — being 3 squares away is fine, 30 squares is not 10× better)
  • $f_5$ = distance to the nearest scared ghost (negative — now you want to chase it)
  • $f_6$ = number of power capsules left (negative: an unused capsule is wasted value)

Note $f_4$ and $f_5$ have opposite signs on the same underlying measurement, which is why a single "ghost distance" feature fails. And note that the sign of $f_3$ is negative while $f_1$ is positive even though they are nearly redundant — redundant features with tuned weights are fine in a linear EVAL; it is missing features that kill you.

What a bad evaluation function does to you

The most famous failure mode is the horizon effect. beyond syllabus Suppose your engine is about to lose a bishop — permanently, in three plies, nothing can stop it. It also has the option of throwing away a pawn to check the enemy king, which delays the bishop's capture by four plies. Now the loss of the bishop is past the horizon, and the truncated tree says: "sacrifice the pawn, position looks fine." The engine will throw away pawn after pawn to push the inevitable just past where it can see, and lose worse than if it had accepted the bishop loss immediately. The evaluation function isn't wrong about any position it is asked about — it is simply never asked about the position that matters.

The standard fix is quiescence search: do not call EVAL at a "noisy" position where captures are pending; extend the search along forcing moves until things settle down, then evaluate. beyond syllabus The lesson generalises beyond chess: a bounded agent's blind spot is exactly where an adversary will aim.

Your chess engine gets twice as fast. Which is not a sensible way to spend the speedup?
Why: Answering faster is worth nothing unless you are losing on the clock — the strength of a bounded-rational agent is set by how much computation it uses, not by how fast it finishes. The other three all convert speed into playing strength, and choosing between them is exactly the feature-complexity vs computation-complexity tradeoff.

Generalized minimax: non-zero-sum and multiplayer games

What if the game is not zero-sum, or has more than two players? The zero-sum bookkeeping miracle fails: with three players you cannot recover players 2 and 3's utilities from player 1's. So you stop trying.

Definition

Generalized minimax. For an $n$-player game:

  • Terminals hold utility tuples $(u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n)$ — one payoff per player.
  • Node values are also tuples, of the same shape.
  • At a node where it is player $i$'s turn, the node's value is the child tuple whose $i$-th component is largest. Player $i$ maximises its own component and is indifferent to everyone else's.

Crucially the node inherits the whole tuple of the winning child, not just the component that was maximised — because the players above still need to know what they get if play goes down here.

Two-player zero-sum is a special case: with $u_2 = -u_1$, "player 2 maximises $u_2$" is literally "player 2 minimises $u_1$," and you can drop the second component. Minimax is generalized minimax with a redundancy removed.

The interesting consequence: cooperation and competition arise dynamically, without being programmed in. Nobody tells the agents to form an alliance. It just happens that at some nodes, the move that is best for player 2 also happens to be good for player 1, and at other nodes it does not. Watch it happen in the example below — players 1 and 2 have identical payoffs everywhere in this tree, so they behave like partners against player 3 for free.

Intuition

Look at what just happened. Player 1 and player 2 never negotiated, yet both chose moves that steered toward $(8,8,1)$ and away from player 3's preferred $(0,0,7)$ and $(0,0,8)$. That is an alliance, emergent from self-interest, because their payoffs happen to be aligned in this tree. Change one leaf so player 2 prefers $(0,0,7)$ and the alliance dissolves immediately — nothing in the algorithm changes. This is why general-sum games are genuinely harder to reason about: the coalition structure is a consequence of the numbers, not an input.

Common mistake

Do not maximise every component, and do not propagate only the component that was maximised. Node 2 in player 3's row is $(8,8,1)$, not $1$ and not $(9,9,1)$. The tuple travels intact.

Alpha-beta pruning

Minimax visits every node. But the worked example already showed that most of them do not matter: once MIN's left subtree revealed a 3, everything else in that subtree was irrelevant. α-β pruning is the systematic version of that observation. It computes exactly the same root value as minimax while often skipping most of the tree.

Definition

Two numbers are carried down the recursion along the current path:

  • $\alpha$ = MAX's best option so far on the path to the root. A value MAX has already secured somewhere above; a lower bound on what the root is worth. Starts at $-\infty$.
  • $\beta$ = MIN's best option so far on the path to the root. A value MIN has already secured above; an upper bound. Starts at $+\infty$.

$\alpha$ only ever increases as you go down a path; $\beta$ only ever decreases. The interval $[\alpha, \beta]$ is the window of values that could still matter at the root. If a node's value provably falls outside the window, the rest of that node is not worth computing.

What the cutoff test actually means

This is the part everybody memorises and nobody understands, so here it is in slow motion. Consider a MIN node $n$ we are in the middle of evaluating.

Facts we have: (i) MIN will take the minimum over $n$'s children, so whatever value $n$ ends up with, it is $\le$ anything we have seen so far at $n$. $n$'s value can only go down as we examine more children. (ii) Somewhere on the path above us there is a MAX node that already has an alternative move worth $\alpha$.

Now suppose at $n$ we compute a running value $v \le \alpha$. Then $n$'s final value is $\le v \le \alpha$. So the MAX node above will compare "at most $\alpha$" (coming through $n$) with "$\alpha$, guaranteed" (the alternative it already has) — and it will never prefer $n$. MAX is never going to come down here. The remaining children of $n$ cannot change that, because they can only lower $n$ further. So stop and return $v$ immediately.

The mirror image, at a MAX node $n$: its value can only go up as we see more children. If a running $v \ge \beta$, then $n$'s final value is $\ge \beta$, and the MIN node above already has an option worth $\beta$, so MIN will never choose to come down here either. Stop.

Analogy

You are hiring, and candidate A has already accepted a $\$120$k offer... no. Better: you are comparing apartments, and you've committed to taking one that costs $\$1200$ (that's your $\alpha$ — the best you've secured). Your landlord-adversary shows you a second building and starts listing units: unit 1 costs $\$1400$. You already know the landlord will quote you the worst unit in that building — wait, invert it: your adversary chooses which unit you get, so you'll get the most expensive one available...

Cleanest version: you're buying a car and the dealer picks which car you get from whatever lot you walk into. You already have a deal worth $\$1200$ of value. You walk into a second lot, and the first car the dealer could stick you with is worth $\$900$. You can leave right now. You don't need to see the rest of the lot, because the dealer is going to give you the worst car there, which is at most $\$900$, which is worse than the $\$1200$ you already hold. Any other car on that lot is information you will never use. That "you can leave right now" is if v <= α: return v.

The exact pseudocode

From the slides, verbatim. Note that α-β does need the two cases written separately, because the cutoff test and the bound update are mirror images:

α: MAX's best option on path to root
β: MIN's best option on path to root

def max-value(state, α, β):
    initialize v = -∞
    for each successor of state:
        v = max(v, value(successor, α, β))
        if v ≥ β
            return v            # cutoff: MIN above won't allow this node
        α = max(α, v)
    return v

def min-value(state, α, β):
    initialize v = +∞
    for each successor of state:
        v = min(v, value(successor, α, β))
        if v ≤ α
            return v            # cutoff: MAX above won't come down here
        β = min(β, v)
    return v

Line by line for max-value: v is the best child value found so far at this node, starting at $-\infty$ so the first child always improves it. For each successor we recurse (passing the current window down). After updating v we test v ≥ β: if our value has already reached the level MIN can avoid, quit. Otherwise we raise α — because if MAX ends up choosing this node, MAX has now secured at least v, and every deeper node should know that.

Common mistake

The order of the three statements inside the loop is not negotiable: update v, then test the cutoff, then update the bound. Updating $\alpha$ before testing would compare $v$ against a $\beta$... no — worse: at a MIN node, updating $\beta$ before testing v ≤ α is harmless, but testing before updating v does nothing, and updating $\alpha$ inside min-value (instead of $\beta$) is the classic bug. In max-value you test against $\beta$ and update $\alpha$. In min-value you test against $\alpha$ and update $\beta$. You always test against the other player's bound and update your own.

Two tiny examples, to drill the tests

max-value cutoff

We are at a MAX node that was called with β = 10. Its first child returns 10, so $v = 10$. Test: is $v \ge \beta$? $10 \ge 10$ — yes, return immediately. The second child (which happens to be worth 100) is never examined.

Why: the MIN node above already has a move worth 10. This node is worth at least 10 and can only grow. MIN will take its 10 elsewhere and never enter here, so the 100 is unreachable. Note the cutoff fires on equality — a tie is enough, because we only need to know MIN has an option at least as good.

min-value cutoff

We are at a MIN node called with α = 10, whose children are 10, 100, 2 left to right. Child 1: $v = 10$; is $10 \le 10$? Yes — cutoff, return 10. The 100 and the 2 are never examined.

Why: MAX already holds 10 above. This node is worth at most 10, so MAX gains nothing by coming here. And look what we skipped: the 2, which would have been this node's true value. We returned 10 when the true value is 2. That is allowed, and it is the subject of the properties section below.

Properties of α-β: what it does and doesn't guarantee

Proof

Theorem. α-β pruning has no effect on the minimax value computed for the root.

Sketch, by induction on the tree. Claim: a call value(n, α, β) returns the exact minimax value $V(n)$ whenever $\alpha < V(n) < \beta$; and when it returns early it returns some $v$ that is on the correct side of the window ($v \le \alpha$ when the true value is $\le \alpha$, or $v \ge \beta$ when the true value is $\ge \beta$).

Base case: a leaf returns its utility with no window involved. Inductive step at a MIN node $n$: the loop computes running minima of exact child values (by induction, as long as those children were called inside a window containing their values). If the loop finishes, $v = \min_i V(c_i) = V(n)$ exactly. If it exits early at child $k$ with $v \le \alpha$, then the true $V(n) = \min_i V(c_i) \le v \le \alpha$, so the returned $v$ is an upper bound on the true value that is still $\le \alpha$ — and the MAX parent, which is comparing against something it has already secured worth $\alpha$, takes $\max(\alpha, \text{something} \le \alpha) = \alpha$ either way. The early return therefore cannot change the parent's choice. MAX nodes are symmetric.

At the root we call with $\alpha = -\infty, \beta = +\infty$, and no finite value lies outside that window, so the root always runs its loop to completion with exact children. Hence the root value is exact. $\blacksquare$

Common mistake

The theorem is about the root only. Values computed at interior nodes can be wrong — and the min-value example above is a live case: we returned 10 for a node whose true minimax value is 2. The returned number is a bound, not a value. Consequences that bite in practice: (1) if you ask for "the minimax value of this interior node" from an α-β run, you may get a bound; (2) an engine that stores α-β results in a transposition table must record whether each entry is exact, a lower bound, or an upper bound. On an exam, if asked for the value at a pruned node, the honest answer is "α-β returns $x$ here, which is only a bound — the true value could be lower."

Move ordering changes everything

The order of generation matters: more pruning is possible if good moves come first. Re-run the classic example with node A's leaves in the order $[8, 12, 3]$ and less gets pruned; put B's 2 last and B's cutoff never fires at all. In the worst case (worst possible ordering) α-β prunes nothing and costs $O(b^m)$, exactly like plain minimax.

But we cannot order moves by their values — computing the values is the whole problem. So we guess, using cheap heuristics: try captures first in chess, try the move that was best at the previous depth, try killer moves that caused cutoffs in sibling nodes.

That "best at the previous depth" trick is why iterative deepening helps. Run the search to depth 1, then 2, then 3, and so on; each pass is cheap relative to the next (the last level dominates the node count), and each pass leaves behind a good guess at the best move to try first in the next pass. The re-searching looks wasteful and is overwhelmingly repaid by the extra pruning it enables. It also gives you an anytime algorithm: when the clock runs out you always have a complete answer from the last finished depth.

Proof

With perfect ordering, time drops to $O(b^{m/2})$.

Suppose at every node the best move really is generated first. To establish the root's value $v$ we need two things, and the tree we must examine is exactly the union of the evidence for them.

To prove the root is worth at least $v$, we exhibit a strategy for MAX: at each MAX node, one move (the good one) — we need not look at the others, because we are proving existence. At each MIN node, we must check all $b$ replies, because the claim is "whatever MIN does, we still get $v$." Alternating, that is $1 \times b \times 1 \times b \times \cdots$ over $m$ levels, i.e. $b^{m/2}$ leaves.

To prove the root is worth at most $v$, the symmetric argument: all $b$ moves at MAX nodes, one refutation at each MIN node — another $b^{m/2}$ leaves.

Total $\Theta(b^{m/2}) = \Theta\!\left((\sqrt{b})^{\,m}\right)$. The effective branching factor becomes $\sqrt{b}$. Since the exponent $m$ is halved for the same node budget, the solvable depth doubles. $\blacksquare$

Redo the chess budget with this. Still $10^6$ nodes per move, still $b \approx 35$, but now $\sqrt{35} \approx 5.92$:

$$5.92^{\,d} = 10^6 \;\Longrightarrow\; d = \frac{6}{\log_{10} 5.92} = \frac{6}{0.772} \approx 7.8 \approx 8$$

Or directly: $b^{m/2} = 35^{4} \approx 10^6$ gives $m = 8$. 1M nodes/move ⟹ depth 8, which the slides call "respectable." Same hardware, same evaluation function, four extra plies — purely from refusing to examine nodes that cannot matter. In practice α-β with good ordering is worth several hundred Elo points, which is the difference between a strong club player and a grandmaster.

True or false: α-β pruning is an approximation that trades accuracy for speed.
Why: α-β is exact. It prunes only subtrees it has proved cannot affect the root, which is why the theorem holds for any move ordering; ordering affects only how much gets pruned, never correctness. The approximations in a real engine come from the depth limit and the evaluation function, not from α-β. And it certainly does not examine every node — skipping nodes is the entire point.

Expectimax: games with chance, and opponents who aren't optimal

Minimax rests on one assumption: the other node is a minimiser. Two very common situations break it.

  • Genuine chance. Backgammon rolls dice; Monopoly rolls dice; Tetris deals a random piece; a card game shuffles. The node after your move is not chosen by anybody — it is sampled. Calling it a minimiser means assuming the dice hate you personally.
  • Suboptimal opponents. A beginner, a random ghost, a distracted human, a rule-based bot. Assuming they play the single most damaging move available is simply a false model of them.

As the notes put it: making worst-case assumptions about stochastic or suboptimal opponents leads to poor decisions. Not merely conservative decisions — poor ones. If you believe every unlikely disaster is certain, you will refuse opportunities that are nearly free, and you will rank two bad-looking options by their worst cases when their averages say the opposite.

Probability refresher

Definition

A random variable represents an event whose outcome is unknown. A probability distribution is an assignment of weights to the possible outcomes, and probabilities over all possible outcomes sum to one.

The course's example: traffic on the freeway. The random variable is $T$ = whether there's traffic; the outcomes are $T \in \{\text{none}, \text{light}, \text{heavy}\}$; the distribution is

$$P(T = \text{none}) = 0.25, \qquad P(T = \text{light}) = 0.50, \qquad P(T = \text{heavy}) = 0.25$$

Check the normalisation: $0.25 + 0.50 + 0.25 = 1$. Always check. If your weights don't sum to one you either forgot an outcome or you are not holding a distribution. Note the capital $T$ is the variable (the uncertain quantity) and lower-case values like "light" are its outcomes; $P(T=t)$ is read "the probability that $T$ takes the value $t$."

Expected value

Definition

The expected value of a function of a random variable is the average of that function's values, weighted by the probability of each outcome:

$$\E_X\big[f(X)\big] \;=\; \sum_x P(X = x)\, f(x)$$

Symbols: $X$ is the random variable; $x$ ranges over all its possible outcomes; $P(X=x)$ is the weight of outcome $x$; $f(x)$ is the number you care about when outcome $x$ happens. The subscript on $\E_X$ says which variable we are averaging over.

The airport example. Let $f(\text{traffic})$ be the time it takes to get to the airport:

Traffic $T$nonelightheavy
Probability $P(T=t)$0.250.500.25
$f(t)$ = travel time20 min30 min60 min
$$\E_T[f(T)] = \sum_t P(T=t) f(t) = 0.25 \times 20 \;+\; 0.50 \times 30 \;+\; 0.25 \times 60$$ $$= 5 \;+\; 15 \;+\; 15 \;=\; \mathbf{35 \text{ minutes}}$$

Three observations worth making on such a small example. (1) $35$ is not a possible outcome — you will never take exactly 35 minutes. An expectation is a summary, not a prediction. (2) $35$ is larger than the median outcome (30), pulled up by the heavy-traffic tail. Expectations are sensitive to rare extremes in a way that minimax is not and medians are not. (3) The pessimist's answer is 60 and the optimist's is 20; both are answers to different questions ("worst case?" and "best case?"). Expectimax answers "on average?"

The expectimax node rules

We model chance as a player in the game tree. Instead of $s'$ being the deterministic result of taking action $a$, $s'$ is one of several possible outcomes, each with a probability. Chance nodes are drawn as circles. The three node types then read:

Key idea

$$\text{MAX nodes:}\quad V(s) = \max_{a} V(s'), \qquad s' = \text{result}(s,a)$$ $$\text{MIN nodes:}\quad V(s) = \min_{a} V(s'), \qquad s' = \text{result}(s,a)$$ $$\text{CHANCE nodes:}\quad V(s) = \sum_{s'} P(s')\, V(s')$$

Same tree, same recursion, one new kind of node. A chance node does not choose — it averages. And notice the chance rule is the expected-value formula from above, with $f$ replaced by "the value of the resulting state."

Names: expectimax for MAX + CHANCE (you against a random world, no adversary — Tetris, investing); expectiminimax for MAX + MIN + CHANCE (an adversary and dice — backgammon, Monopoly); plain minimax for MAX + MIN (tic-tac-toe, chess). Same code, different node types present.

Key idea

We select the action leading to the highest expected value, not the highest individual outcome. The single biggest number in Figure 6 is a 12, and there are two of them — one under Left and one under Right. Neither is the reason Right wins. Right wins because its worst outcome (6) is decent and carries most of the weight. Expectimax rewards the shape of the whole distribution, not its best point.

Common mistake

Running a min or a max at a chance node. A chance node is not a player, so it never chooses; and it is not a MAX node in disguise, so its value can be lower than every... no — its value always lies between its smallest and largest child, strictly inside unless one outcome has probability 1. If you ever compute a chance value outside that range, you have an arithmetic error, and the usual cause is probabilities that don't sum to one.

Step 4 — The same tree under minimax

Now the outcomes are an opponent's choices and the probabilities are ignored entirely:

(a) Chance values $9, 5, 7$; expectimax plays Left, value 9.

(b) Minimax values $4, 0, 6$; minimax plays Right, value 6.

(c) "Chase the biggest number" picks Center, because a 20 is sitting there. Center is the worst action under both models — worst expectation (5) and worst worst-case (0). A single large outcome at low probability is a trap under every sane decision rule.

They answer different questions because they encode different beliefs about who or what controls those nodes. Left is right if the outcomes really are coin flips; Right is right if something is choosing them against you. Choosing between the two is a modelling decision made before any search happens — which is the subject of the last section.

Can you prune a chance node?

The slides raise the question — "Expectimax pruning?" over a tree with leaves 3, 12, 9, 2 — and leave it hanging. Here is the answer.

In general, no: you cannot prune at a chance node without bounds on the utilities. The reason is structural. α-β works because a MIN node's running value can only go down and a MAX node's can only go up. Those are monotone one-sided guarantees, and a one-sided guarantee is exactly what lets you say "this can never beat what I already hold."

A chance node has no such property. Its value is $\sum_{s'} P(s') V(s')$, a weighted average that partial information does not bound. Suppose a chance node has two equally likely outcomes and you have evaluated the first at 3. What do you know about the node? Only that its value is $\tfrac12(3) + \tfrac12(V_2) = 1.5 + 0.5 V_2$ — and with $V_2$ unconstrained, that is any number at all. Seeing a terrible first outcome tells you nothing, because the second could be $+10^9$ and drag the average anywhere. Averages are not monotone in the outcomes you have seen so far.

Now bound the utilities, and it changes. beyond syllabus Suppose every leaf lies in $[L, U]$. After evaluating the first outcome of an $n$-outcome chance node, the best possible completion is to assign $U$ to everything unseen and the worst is to assign $L$:

$$\underbrace{\sum_{\text{seen}} P(s')V(s') + \sum_{\text{unseen}} P(s')\,L}_{\text{lower bound}} \;\le\; V(\text{chance}) \;\le\; \underbrace{\sum_{\text{seen}} P(s')V(s') + \sum_{\text{unseen}} P(s')\,U}_{\text{upper bound}}$$

If the upper bound already falls at or below $\alpha$, MAX will never come here, and you may stop. This is real and it is called *-minimax (star1/star2 pruning, Ballard 1983); backgammon engines use it. But note what it needs: a known, finite range for utilities, and it prunes far less than α-β because the bounds are loose. With unbounded utilities — or with a chance node whose outcomes you evaluate in a bad order — you get nothing.

Intuition

α-β prunes because an adversary is predictable at the extreme: you know MIN will pick the worst thing for you, so one bad option is enough to condemn a node. Chance is unpredictable in the middle: no single outcome condemns anything, because the others vote too. Adversaries are, in this narrow sense, easier to reason about than dice.

Modelling assumptions: know your opponent

Everything above assumed you knew which kind of node you were looking at. What if you get that wrong?

Take the slides' little tree: a MAX root with two options. The left option leads to a node with children $\{10, 10\}$; the right option to a node with children $\{9, 100\}$.

  • If those are MIN nodes: left $= \min(10,10) = 10$, right $= \min(9,100) = 9$. Play left, value 10.
  • If those are chance nodes (say 50/50): left $= 10$, right $= \tfrac12(9) + \tfrac12(100) = 54.5$. Play right, expected value 54.5.

Same numbers, opposite decisions, and the gap is enormous. The slides name the two failure modes:

Dangerous pessimism

Assuming the worst case when it's not likely. You run minimax against a world that is merely random. You pass up the right branch — expected value 54.5 — to collect a guaranteed 10, because you treated a 50/50 coin as an enemy. You are never destroyed, but you leave enormous value on the table, permanently.

Dangerous optimism

Assuming chance when the world is adversarial. You run expectimax against a real opponent. You take the right branch expecting 54.5 and the opponent, who was never rolling dice, hands you the 9. Worse, in a real game those "9"s are the losses, so you do not merely underperform — you get punished specifically for the optimism, over and over, because a competent opponent steers toward exactly the outcomes your model called unlikely.

Analogy

The minimax driver. Imagine an autonomous vehicle that runs minimax against "the other cars." Minimax says: every oncoming car might swerve into my lane, every pedestrian might step out, so assume they will. The worst case of driving anywhere is a fatal collision, and the worst case of staying in the driveway is zero. A minimax driver never leaves the driveway. Real driving requires expectimax: other drivers are stochastic, mostly-cooperative agents, and you must weigh probabilities or you cannot move at all.

Where the analogy breaks: you do not want pure expectimax either. A pedestrian with a foot off the kerb deserves something closer to worst-case treatment. Real systems use expectimax over a learned behaviour model, with hard minimax-style safety constraints bolted on top — which is exactly "dangerous optimism" being engineered around.

Games force efficient use of computation — α-β exists because the budget is finite. And game playing has produced ideas that outlived the games:

  • Reinforcement learning came out of checkers (Samuel's self-play evaluation tuning).
  • Iterative deepening came out of chess engineering.
  • Monte Carlo tree search came out of Go.
  • Solution methods for partial-information games came out of poker, and feed straight back into economics.

And the frontier is not solved: video games present much greater challenges, with $b = 10^{500}$, $|S| = 10^{4000}$ and $m = 10{,}000$ — numbers that make chess look like tic-tac-toe.

Intuition

Where this goes next. Look again at the chance-node rule and the max rule together:

$$V(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s,a)\, V(s')$$

That is expectimax written as one equation — and it is one small step from the Bellman equation for a Markov decision process:

$$V(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s,a)\big[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V(s')\big]$$

with value iteration $V_{k+1}(s) = \max_a \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V_k(s')]$, Q-iteration $Q_{k+1}(s,a) = \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)[R(s,a,s') + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_k(s',a')]$, and policy extraction $\pi_V(s) = \argmax_a \sum_{s'} P(s'\mid s,a)[R(s,a,s') + \gamma V(s')]$. The differences are: rewards arrive along the way rather than only at the end, a discount $\gamma$ keeps infinite games finite, and states repeat so you iterate to a fixed point instead of recursing down a tree.
