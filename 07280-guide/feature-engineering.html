Part II · Chapter 10

Feature engineering & overfitting

How to make a straight-line model fit a curve without changing the model at all — and the exact price you pay for overdoing it.

1. The premise: simple models, complex data

Here is the sentence the course notes open with, unpacked:

Key idea

If your dataset is complex — meaning the input values have complicated relationships with the output values — then simple models may be unable to fit it. Feature engineering is the tool that lets simple models fit complex distributions of data.

Read that twice, because the phrasing is doing something sneaky. It does not say "use a more complex model." It says: keep the simple model, change the data. That is the whole chapter.

You already have a simple model. From chapter 8, for a one-dimensional input $x \in \R$:

$$ h(x) = wx + b $$

where $w$ is the slope (how much the prediction rises per unit of $x$) and $b$ is the intercept or bias (the prediction when $x = 0$). You fit it by minimising squared error, and you learned that there is a closed-form solution — the normal equations. It is interpretable (one number per feature, and you can read its sign), it is easy to optimise (convex, closed form), and it is simple (two parameters).

Now watch it fail.

Intuition

Here is the tension the notes name explicitly. The linearity constraint is what makes linear regression good: it is easily interpretable, easy to optimise, and simple. But that same constraint is what makes it a poor model for complex, non-linear distributions. Ordinarily you would expect to trade one for the other — give up interpretability to get flexibility.

Feature engineering is the move that refuses the trade. You keep interpretability, keep the closed-form optimisation, keep the simplicity — and still fit the curve.

2. The trick: linear in the parameters, not in the input

Look at the data again. It "strongly resembles a quadratic curve," as the notes put it. So suppose we had a model that learned optimal parameters $[b, w_1, w_2]^\top$ for

$$ h(x) = b + w_1 x + w_2 x^2 $$

Then we could represent a quadratic distribution and fit the data well. Fine. But how? We only know how to fit lines.

Here is the answer, and it is the most important sentence on this page:

Key idea

$h(x) = b + w_1x + w_2x^2$ is non-linear with respect to the input $x$, but it is still a linear function with respect to the parameters $(b, w_1, w_2)$.

Linear regression does not care what the inputs are. It only cares that the prediction is a weighted sum of the parameters. So we can apply linear regression — we just have to hand it an extra column.

2.1 Why "linear in the parameters" is the property that matters

Let me belabour this, because it is the thing students memorise without understanding, and then they get stuck the first time they meet a model that isn't linear in its parameters.

Go back to what linear regression actually is. It is an optimisation problem:

$$ \theta^* = \argmin_{\theta} \; \sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y^{(i)} - \theta^\top z^{(i)}\big)^2 $$

where $\theta \in \R^M$ is the parameter vector and $z^{(i)} \in \R^M$ is whatever numbers we choose to put in row $i$. Name every symbol: $N$ is the number of samples, $M$ is the number of numbers per row, $y^{(i)}$ is the $i$-th target, $\theta^\top z^{(i)}$ is the dot product $\sum_{j} \theta_j z^{(i)}_j$ — the prediction.

Now ask: what makes this problem solvable in closed form? Two facts, and only two:

  1. The prediction $\theta^\top z^{(i)}$ is a linear function of $\theta$.
  2. The loss is the square of (target − prediction), so the objective is a quadratic in $\theta$, hence convex, hence its gradient is linear in $\theta$, hence setting the gradient to zero gives a linear system you can solve.

Notice what is absent from that list: any requirement whatsoever on $z^{(i)}$. The numbers in $z^{(i)}$ are constants as far as the optimisation is concerned. They are given. They were computed before the optimiser ever ran. It makes no difference to the algebra whether $z^{(i)} = [1, x^{(i)}]^\top$ or $z^{(i)} = [1, x^{(i)}, (x^{(i)})^2]^\top$ or $z^{(i)} = [1, \sin(x^{(i)}), e^{x^{(i)}}, \log(1 + (x^{(i)})^2)]^\top$.

Analogy

A vending machine takes coins and returns a total. It is "linear in the coins": two quarters is twice one quarter. It does not care where the quarters came from — your pocket, a bank, a fountain. The machine's linearity is a property of how it combines its inputs, not of how the inputs were produced.

Linear regression is the machine. The parameters $\theta$ are the quantities being combined linearly. The features $z$ are the coin denominations — fixed constants, chosen in advance by you. You may mint whatever denominations you like, including a coin worth $x^2$ dollars, and the machine still adds them up linearly.

Where the analogy breaks: in the vending machine the coins are given to you; in ML, choosing the denominations is the job, and choosing badly is how you overfit. Part 6 of this page is about exactly that.

Common mistake

"Linear regression can only fit straight lines." False. Linear regression can fit any function that is a linear combination of features you supply. The word "linear" in "linear regression" refers to linearity in the parameters, not linearity of the resulting curve in $x$.

A model that is genuinely not linear in its parameters would be something like $h(x) = b + w_1 e^{w_2 x}$ — here $w_2$ sits inside an exponential, the objective stops being quadratic in the parameters, and no normal-equation trick saves you. That is the boundary of what this chapter buys you.

3.1 The warning: the same transformation at prediction time

The notes say this twice, and here is the third time: the transformation is part of the model now.

The curve you just drew still represents the predictions of a linear regression model that takes in samples from $\D$ — the original dataset — as input. But when you predict on a new sample $x \in \R$, you must first engineer the feature $x^2$, and only then feed $[1, x, x^2]$ to the model.

Example

A concrete failure story. You train the quadratic model above and ship it. Training code lives in train.py; serving code lives in predict.py, written three weeks later by someone else who has the fitted vector $\theta = [15.354,\; -7.712,\; 1.021]$ and the note "it takes $x$."

A request arrives: $x = 4$. The correct answer is

$$ h(4) = 15.354 - 7.712(4) + 1.021(16) = 15.354 - 30.849 + 16.334 = \mathbf{0.840} $$

Failure mode 1 — the column is missing. predict.py builds $[1, 4]$, the framework pads with a zero, and you compute $15.354 - 30.849 + 0 = \mathbf{-15.50}$. The true target near $x=4$ is about $1$. You are off by 16 on a scale where the whole dataset spans 1 to 15. And the prediction is negative, which for a count, a price or a duration is not merely wrong but impossible.

Failure mode 2 — the column is there but wrong. Someone writes [1, x, x] instead of [1, x, x*x]. Now you compute $15.354 - 30.849 + 4.084 = \mathbf{-11.41}$. Still catastrophic — and it will not crash. The shapes match. The code runs. The dashboard shows numbers. This class of bug is silent, which is why it survives to production.

Failure mode 3 — the transform differs in a subtler way. Training standardised $x$ before squaring (so the feature is $((x-\mu)/s)^2$) and serving squares the raw $x$. Every prediction is wrong by a smooth, plausible-looking amount. You will find this one with a data drift alarm, six weeks later, if you are lucky.

The fix: make $\phi$ a single function that both code paths call. Never re-implement it. In practice this is why libraries wrap the transform and the model together in one "pipeline" object that is fitted and saved as a unit.

Guided problemEngineer the feature, fit the model, predict

You are given three samples of $\D$: $(x, y) = (0, 15),\ (2, 5),\ (6, 5)$. Fit $h(x) = b + w_1x + w_2x^2$ exactly (three parameters, three points — expect an exact fit), then predict at $x = 3$.

Step 1 — Build $\D'$

Add the $x^2$ column: $(x, x^2, y) = (0, 0, 15),\ (2, 4, 5),\ (6, 36, 5)$.

The design matrix, with the column of ones for the bias, is

$$ \Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 6 & 36 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad y = \begin{bmatrix} 15 \\ 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} $$

Three rows, three columns — square. With three distinct $x$ values this matrix is invertible, so we can solve $\Phi\theta = y$ directly rather than going through the normal equations. (This is the special case $M = N$; §7 says what happens when it goes further.)

Step 2 — Solve the linear system

Row 1: $b + 0 + 0 = 15 \Rightarrow b = 15$.

Row 2: $15 + 2w_1 + 4w_2 = 5 \Rightarrow 2w_1 + 4w_2 = -10 \Rightarrow w_1 + 2w_2 = -5$.

Row 3: $15 + 6w_1 + 36w_2 = 5 \Rightarrow 6w_1 + 36w_2 = -10 \Rightarrow 3w_1 + 18w_2 = -5$.

Multiply the second equation by 3: $3w_1 + 6w_2 = -15$. Subtract from the third: $12w_2 = 10 \Rightarrow w_2 = \tfrac{5}{6} \approx 0.8333$.

Back-substitute: $w_1 = -5 - 2(\tfrac56) = -5 - \tfrac{5}{3} = -\tfrac{20}{3} \approx -6.6667$.

Step 3 — Predict at $x = 3$, correctly and incorrectly

Correctly. Engineer first: $x = 3 \Rightarrow x^2 = 9$. Then

$$ h(3) = 15 - \tfrac{20}{3}(3) + \tfrac56(9) = 15 - 20 + 7.5 = \mathbf{2.5} $$

The true $y$ at $x=3$ in the full dataset is $1$, so we are off by $1.5$ — reasonable, given we fitted on only three points.

Incorrectly. Forget the transform and feed $[1, 3, 0]$: $15 - 20 + 0 = -5$. Feed $[1,3,3]$ by mistake: $15 - 20 + 2.5 = -2.5$. Both are negative; the data never goes below $1$.

Answer & the takeaway

$b = 15$, $w_1 = -20/3$, $w_2 = 5/6$, and $h(3) = 2.5$.

Takeaway: fitting a "curve" required zero new machinery — one extra column and a $3\times3$ linear solve. And the model you shipped is not $\theta$ alone; it is the pair $(\phi, \theta)$. Lose $\phi$ and $\theta$ is meaningless numbers.

4. Another perspective: you are fitting a plane, not a curve

This is the section (the notes call it an "Aside") that turns memorisation into understanding, so slow down here.

Our original dataset $\D$ had one feature. The linear regression model we trained was trained on the two features of $\D'$. Because we are still working with 2-D inputs plus a 1-D output, we can still draw the whole thing — in three dimensions.

And here is the punchline: because the model has two features, it is not fitting a line to the dataset. It is fitting a plane.

Intuition

Take the five points, which in 1-D live on a curve. Give each one a second coordinate equal to $x^2$. That coordinate lifts each point out of the $x$–$y$ page and into a third dimension. Where do the lifted points land? Along the surface $x_2 = x^2$ — a parabolic sheet standing perpendicular to the floor.

Now look at the lifted points. They are no longer U-shaped in any plane that matters: they lie on a flat sheet. A plane can pass through all of them. The curvature did not disappear — it got absorbed into the position of the points in the new coordinate, leaving the relationship linear.

The curve in 1-D is a plane in 2-D feature space. Same object, two drawings.

Figure 3 (reconstructed) — lifting $\D$ into $(x, x^2, y)$ space
Reconstruction of the notes' Figure 3. The floor is the feature space $(x, x^2)$; height is $y$. Every sample is forced onto the purple parabola on the floor — that is the only place a point with coordinates $(x, x^2)$ can be. Lift each one to its $y$ value (orange dots) and they line up on a flat teal sheet: the plane $y = 15.354 - 7.712x + 1.021x_2$. The orange curve is nothing but the plane's height read off along the purple parabola — i.e. the parabola you saw in the previous figure. Common mistake

"So the model can also predict at $(x, x_2) = (3, 100)$?" Algebraically yes; meaningfully no. Your engineered features are not free coordinates. A real sample always satisfies $x_2 = x^2$, so all genuine data lies on the purple parabola, a one-dimensional curve inside a two-dimensional space. The plane is defined everywhere, but it is only ever evaluated on that curve, and it was only ever fitted near it. Off the curve, the model's output is an extrapolation with no support whatsoever.

This is worth remembering because it is the reason high-degree polynomial features are so dangerous: the data occupies a thin, curved sliver of a very high-dimensional space, and almost all of that space is unconstrained.

In the lifted picture, what geometric object is the trained model?
Why: The model is $y = b + w_1x + w_2x_2$ — an affine function of two variables, which is a flat plane, full stop. Nothing about it is curved. The curvature you see when you plot $h$ against $x$ comes entirely from the constraint $x_2 = x^2$ that real inputs obey, which traces a parabolic path across the flat plane. Flat surface, curved path along it.

5. Generalising: polynomial feature transforms

To model a quadratic, we engineered $x^2$. Suppose we now want a cubic,

$$ h(x) = b + w_1x + w_2x^2 + w_3x^3 $$

or a quartic (degree-4 polynomial),

$$ h(x) = b + w_1x + w_2x^2 + w_3x^3 + w_4x^4 $$

Then all we have to do is engineer the features for the higher-powered terms, and $h$ can model those polynomials. In general, to model a degree-$M$ polynomial on a one-feature dataset $\D$, add columns as below. While we're at it, we insert a column of ones to account for the bias term $b$ — from now on the bias is not a special case, it is just the weight on a feature that is always 1.

Common mistake

Notation alert (the notes flag this, and here is why it matters so much right here): $x^{(i)}$ is the $i$-th sample, not an exponent.

Look at the cell $\big(x^{(3)}\big)^2$. It contains both kinds of superscript at once: the parenthesised $(3)$ says "third sample", the bare $2$ says "squared". Read it as "the third sample, squared." The outer parentheses in the table are there precisely to keep the two apart — without them, $x^{(3)2}$ is unreadable.

A quick test you can always run: is the superscript in parentheses? Parentheses ⇒ index. No parentheses ⇒ power. So $x^{(2)}$ is sample two, $x^2$ is $x$ squared, and $\big(x^{(2)}\big)^2$ is sample two squared. Rows are indexed by $(i)$; columns are indexed by powers.

Definition

Polynomial feature transform. The generalisation of feature engineering to $M$-th degree polynomials, in which the dataset is augmented to include polynomial terms $1, x, x^2, \dots, x^M$. Each sample of $\D$ contributes one row of $M+1$ numbers.

Two things to notice before we move on. First, the number of columns grows by one per degree, so a degree-$M$ transform on a one-dimensional input costs $M+1$ parameters. Second — and this bites later — if your original input has $K$ features rather than one, and you take all monomials up to degree $M$, the column count is $\binom{K+M}{M}$, which explodes. Degree 3 on 100 input features is 176,851 columns. Keep that number in mind for §6.

6. Abstract feature transforms: $\phi$ and $\Phi$

So far every transformation has been "raise something to a power." That is just a special case. Instead of applying an exponent, we could have chosen any function we desired:

  • Periodic: apply $\sin$ to get a new feature $\sin(x)$. Essential when the signal has a cycle — hour of day, day of year, phase of a rotating machine. Pair it with $\cos(x)$ so that a phase shift is representable as a linear combination.
  • Radial: include the distance from the origin $\lVert x\rVert_2 = \sqrt{x_1^2 + \dots + x_K^2}$ as a feature. This alone lets a linear model carve out a circle in the original space — because "$\lVert x\rVert_2 \le r$" is a linear constraint in the new coordinate.
  • Logs: $\log(1+x)$ compresses heavy-tailed quantities (income, population, view counts) so a handful of giants stop dominating the squared loss.
  • Interactions: $x_1x_2$. Without it, a linear model insists the effect of $x_1$ is the same regardless of $x_2$. With it, the model can say "more fertiliser helps, but only if there is water."
  • Indicators / one-hot: for a categorical input such as colour ∈ {red, green, blue}, create three binary columns $\1[\text{red}], \1[\text{green}], \1[\text{blue}]$. This is feature engineering too — and it is how a model that only does arithmetic handles a variable with no arithmetic.
  • Buckets: $\1[x < 0],\ \1[0 \le x < 5],\ \1[x \ge 5]$ turns a continuous feature into a step function the linear model can fit at whatever heights it likes.

The only rule: you may choose any transformation you like, as long as you apply the same transformation to each sample at training time and at prediction time. (Yes — §3.1 again. Third mention in the notes, fourth here.)

6.1 The notation

Let us write this down properly. Suppose we are given a dataset $X \in \R^{N\times K}$, where $N$ is the number of samples and $K$ is the number of features per sample. Define a feature transformation function

$$ \phi : \R^{K} \to \R^{M} $$

where $M$ is the number of features produced by the transformation $\phi$. Then the transformed dataset is written $\Phi$, stacked row by row:

$$ X = \begin{bmatrix} \text{---}\;\; {x^{(1)}}^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \\ \text{---}\;\; {x^{(2)}}^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \\ \text{---}\;\; {x^{(3)}}^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \\ \vdots \\ \text{---}\;\; {x^{(N)}}^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \end{bmatrix} \qquad \Phi = \begin{bmatrix} \text{---}\;\; \phi\big(x^{(1)}\big)^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \\ \text{---}\;\; \phi\big(x^{(2)}\big)^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \\ \text{---}\;\; \phi\big(x^{(3)}\big)^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \\ \vdots \\ \text{---}\;\; \phi\big(x^{(N)}\big)^{\!\top} \;\;\text{---} \end{bmatrix} $$

(often just renamed $X$)

Symbol by symbol: each $x^{(i)} \in \R^K$ is a column vector, so ${x^{(i)}}^\top$ is a row, which is why it becomes row $i$ of the matrix. The long dashes are the notes' way of saying "this row is that whole vector laid flat." Applying $\phi$ turns a $K$-vector into an $M$-vector, so row $i$ of $\Phi$ has $M$ entries.

Therefore $\Phi \in \R^{N \times M}$, because we applied the feature transform $\phi$ to each sample $x^{(i)}$ in the dataset. We now have a fully general notion of feature transformation as a function $\phi$ that machine-learning engineers — that's you — can define to be anything at all.

Common mistake

The parenthetical "often just renamed $X$" is not a typo, and it trips people up in papers and code. Once you have transformed the data, most texts stop distinguishing the two and write $X$ for the transformed matrix, $\theta^* = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$, and so on. So when you read "$X$", ask which $X$ — raw or transformed? In code it is the difference between X_raw and X_poly, and in exams it is the difference between $K$ and $M$ columns.

Example

Example 1 from the notes. Relating back to our original example, when performing a polynomial feature transformation of degree 3, we were using the feature transform

$$ \phi(x) = [\,1,\ x,\ x^2,\ x^3\,]^\top $$

Here $K = 1$ (one raw feature) and $M = 4$ (four engineered features, including the constant). So $\phi: \R^1 \to \R^4$, and for $N$ samples, $\Phi \in \R^{N\times 4}$.

Numerically, on our dataset with $x^{(1)}=0,\ x^{(2)}=2,\ x^{(3)}=3,\ x^{(4)}=6,\ x^{(5)}=7$:

$$ \Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 1 & 2 & 4 & 8\\ 1 & 3 & 9 & 27\\ 1 & 6 & 36 & 216\\ 1 & 7 & 49 & 343\end{bmatrix} \in \R^{5\times 4} $$

Look at the last column: 0, 8, 27, 216, 343. The columns are on wildly different scales. That is a practical warning about polynomial features — numerically they are nasty, and standardising $x$ before you raise it to powers is close to mandatory in real code.

Guided problemDesign $\phi$ for a two-feature dataset

You have $N = 3$ samples with $K = 2$ raw features, $x = (x_1, x_2)$:

Step 1 — Choose the features and count them

$$ \phi(x) = \big[\,1,\ x_1,\ x_2,\ x_1x_2,\ \sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}\,\big]^\top $$

$M = 5$. The map is $\phi : \R^2 \to \R^5$, so $\Phi \in \R^{3\times 5}$.

Step 2 — Evaluate $\phi$ on each sample

$\phi(x^{(1)}) = [1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 1{\cdot}2,\ \sqrt{1+4}]^\top = [1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 2,\ 2.2361]^\top$

$\phi(x^{(2)}) = [1,\ 3,\ 0,\ 3{\cdot}0,\ \sqrt{9+0}]^\top = [1,\ 3,\ 0,\ 0,\ 3]^\top$

$\phi(x^{(3)}) = [1,\ 2,\ -1,\ 2{\cdot}(-1),\ \sqrt{4+1}]^\top = [1,\ 2,\ -1,\ -2,\ 2.2361]^\top$

Step 3 — Stack them as rows
$$ \Phi = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 & 2 & 2.2361 \\ 1 & 3 & 0 & 0 & 3.0000 \\ 1 & 2 & -1 & -2 & 2.2361 \end{bmatrix} $$

Each row is one transformed sample. Each column is one engineered feature across all samples. If you ever write $\phi(x^{(i)})$ as a column of $\Phi$, you have transposed the whole thing and every subsequent formula will have mismatched shapes.

Answer & the takeaway

$M = 5$, $\Phi \in \R^{3\times5}$ as above.

Takeaway — and a deliberate trap: $M = 5$ but $N = 3$. You have five parameters and three equations. There are infinitely many $\theta$ that fit these three points exactly, and $\Phi^\top\Phi \in \R^{5\times5}$ has rank at most 3, so it is singular and $(\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}$ does not exist. That is §7, and it is not a rare pathology — it is what happens every single time you get enthusiastic about features.

7. The normal equations carry over — and where they break

Because nothing about the optimisation changed, everything you derived in chapter 8 transfers with $X$ replaced by $\Phi$. The objective is

$$ J(\theta) \;=\; \lVert y - \Phi\theta\rVert_2^2 \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big(y^{(i)} - \theta^\top \phi\big(x^{(i)}\big)\Big)^2 $$

and setting $\nabla_\theta J = 0$ gives, exactly as before,

$$ \Phi^\top\Phi\,\theta^* = \Phi^\top y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\theta^* = (\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top y\;} $$
Proof

Expand the objective, treating $\Phi$ and $y$ as constants:

$$ J(\theta) = (y - \Phi\theta)^\top(y-\Phi\theta) = y^\top y - 2\,\theta^\top\Phi^\top y + \theta^\top\Phi^\top\Phi\,\theta $$

Line 1 is just the definition of the squared norm. Line 2 multiplies out; the two cross terms $y^\top\Phi\theta$ and $\theta^\top\Phi^\top y$ are $1\times1$ matrices, i.e. scalars, and a scalar equals its own transpose, so they are equal and combine into $-2\theta^\top\Phi^\top y$.

Differentiate with respect to $\theta$, using $\nabla_\theta(\theta^\top a) = a$ and $\nabla_\theta(\theta^\top A\theta) = 2A\theta$ for symmetric $A$ (and $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is symmetric, since $(\Phi^\top\Phi)^\top = \Phi^\top\Phi$):

$$ \nabla_\theta J = -2\Phi^\top y + 2\Phi^\top\Phi\,\theta $$

Set to zero and divide by 2: $\Phi^\top\Phi\,\theta^* = \Phi^\top y$. Since $J$ is a convex quadratic (its Hessian $2\Phi^\top\Phi$ is positive semi-definite, because $v^\top\Phi^\top\Phi v = \lVert\Phi v\rVert^2 \ge 0$ for all $v$), this stationary point is a global minimum. If $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is invertible, multiply on the left by its inverse. ∎

Every step used only that the prediction is $\theta^\top\phi(x)$ — linear in $\theta$. The proof never asked what $\phi$ does. That is the formal version of §2.

7.1 What happens when $M > N$

Chapter 8 raised the corner case "what if $X^\top X$ isn't invertible?" Feature engineering is the place where that corner case becomes the common case, so let us be precise.

$\Phi^\top\Phi$ is $M \times M$. Its rank is $\operatorname{rank}(\Phi) \le \min(N, M)$. So:

  • If $M > N$, then $\operatorname{rank}(\Phi^\top\Phi) \le N < M$, and an $M\times M$ matrix of rank less than $M$ is singular. The inverse does not exist and the boxed formula is meaningless as written.
  • Geometrically: you have $M$ unknowns and only $N$ independent equations. The solution set is an affine subspace of dimension at least $M - N$ — infinitely many parameter vectors that all achieve exactly zero training error.
  • Every one of them interpolates your training data perfectly. They disagree wildly everywhere else. Nothing in the least-squares objective prefers one over another; the criterion you trained on cannot see the difference.

Even at $M = N$ you are already in trouble: with $N$ distinct $x$ values and a degree-$(N-1)$ polynomial, the Vandermonde matrix is square and invertible, so there is a unique fit and it passes through every point exactly. Training error zero. That sounds like success. Section 8 explains why it is a disaster.

And just below that threshold, $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is technically invertible but ill-conditioned — nearly singular — so the computed $\theta$ has enormous entries of alternating sign that nearly cancel. That is precisely what produces the violent wiggles you are about to see.

Key idea

Three fixes exist for $M \ge N$, and you will meet all of them:

  1. Use fewer features — the subject of the rest of this page.
  2. Use the pseudo-inverse $\theta^* = \Phi^{+}y$, which among all the perfect fits returns the one with smallest $\lVert\theta\rVert$. beyond syllabus
  3. Regularise: solve $(\Phi^\top\Phi + \lambda I)\theta = \Phi^\top y$ instead. Adding $\lambda I$ with $\lambda > 0$ makes the matrix strictly positive definite, hence invertible, always. See §10. beyond syllabus

The notes pose the question and then answer it with a picture, so let us do the same. If adding features lets our model generalise to a larger family of functions, why not add as many as we can? What downside is there?

Key idea

Intuition

Think of it as the difference between a student who learns the material and a student who memorises last year's exam. Both score 100% on last year's exam. Exactly one of them can answer a question they have not seen.

Training error is last year's exam. It is the only thing the optimiser can see, and the optimiser will happily memorise if you give it enough capacity to do so. Degree 11 on 12 points is a model with enough memory to store the answer key.

Where the analogy breaks: memorisation sounds like a deliberate strategy, but nothing is being decided here. Least squares is just minimising what you told it to minimise. The failure is yours, in choosing a hypothesis class rich enough that "memorise" and "learn" score identically on the objective.

10. Overfitting, properly

The notes stop at "this is overfitting; more to come later in the course." Let us do the later part now, because you need it to make sense of everything from here to neural networks. extends the reading

10.1 The two error curves

Definition

Training error is the average loss on the data you fitted on. Test error (or generalisation error) is the average loss on data drawn from the same distribution that the model never saw during fitting.

As model capacity grows: training error decreases monotonically (a richer hypothesis class contains the poorer one, so its best member is at least as good), while test error typically falls, reaches a minimum, and then rises. The resulting shape is the classic U.

The canonical picture: error against model complexity
The single most reused diagram in machine learning. On the left both curves are high and close together — the model cannot express the truth, so it fails on training and test data alike. On the right they diverge: the model expresses the noise in the training set, which by definition does not repeat in the test set. The vertical gap between the curves is the quantity you are trying to keep small at the same time as the test curve itself.

10.2 Saying it with risk and empirical risk

In chapter 5 you met two quantities. Here is what overfitting is in that language, which is the precise version.

The true risk (expected loss) of a hypothesis $h$ is an expectation over the data-generating distribution $p(x, y)$:

$$ R(h) \;=\; \E_{(x,y)\sim p}\big[\ell\big(y, h(x)\big)\big] $$

This is what you actually care about: the average loss on every sample you will ever meet. You cannot compute it, because you do not have $p$. So you compute the empirical risk on your $N$ samples instead:

$$ \hat R(h) \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell\big(y^{(i)}, h(x^{(i)})\big) $$

and minimise that — empirical risk minimisation. The entire enterprise rests on $\hat R$ being a good stand-in for $R$.

Key idea

Overfitting is the failure of that stand-in. As capacity grows, $\hat R(h^*)$ keeps falling — all the way to zero — while $R(h^*)$ turns around and rises. The optimiser is doing its job perfectly. The job is just the wrong one.

Precisely: $\hat R$ is an unbiased estimate of $R$ for a hypothesis chosen independently of the sample. But $h^*$ was chosen by minimising $\hat R$ on that very sample, so it is selected partly for fitting the sample's noise. That selection is what makes $\hat R(h^*)$ an optimistically biased estimate of $R(h^*)$, and the bias grows with the number of hypotheses you effectively searched over — i.e. with capacity.

This also tells you why a held-out test set works: those points were not used to choose $h$, so on them $\hat R$ is unbiased again.

10.3 Underfitting, the mirror failure

Overfitting gets all the attention, but Figure 2 at the top of this page was underfitting — a model too rigid to express the truth, with high error on training and test data. The diagnosis is easy and the two are never confused once you look at both numbers:

Training errorTest errorDiagnosisWhat to do
HighHigh (≈ training)UnderfittingMore features, higher degree, richer model, train longer
LowLowGood fitStop. Ship it.

HighLowBugYour split leaked, or the sets came from different distributions

10.4 Bias and variance, in words

Intuition

Imagine re-collecting your training set from scratch many times and re-fitting each time. You get a cloud of fitted functions.

Bias is how far the average of that cloud sits from the truth. A degree-1 model on curved data is biased: every resample gives roughly the same wrong line. Rigid, consistent, consistently wrong.

Variance is how much the members of the cloud differ from each other. A degree-15 model on 10 points has huge variance: shift one point by a hair and the curve rewrites itself between the points. Flexible, unstable, unreliable.

Complexity trades one for the other. Low complexity ⇒ high bias, low variance ⇒ underfitting. High complexity ⇒ low bias, high variance ⇒ overfitting. The test-error curve is the sum of the two plus the irreducible noise, which is why it is U-shaped: one term falls and the other rises.

Dartboard version: bias is the whole group of darts being off-centre; variance is the group being scattered. You can be tight and wrong (high bias, low variance), loose and centred (low bias, high variance), or — the goal — tight and centred.

10.5 The fixes the notes promise are coming

The notes end §1.6 with "there's certainly more to come in this course on how to combat overfitting." Here is the map of what is coming, so you know what each thing is for.

Fix 1

Held-out validation set

Split the data three ways: train / validation / test. Fit on train, pick the degree by validation error, report on test. This is literally the right-hand panel of the widget above, used as a decision procedure. Cost: you spend data on measurement instead of fitting.

Fix 2

$k$-fold cross-validation

Split into $k$ folds; train on $k-1$ and validate on the remaining one, $k$ times; average. Every point is used for both fitting and validation, just never at the same time. Costs $k\times$ the compute and buys you a far less noisy estimate — which matters enormously when $N$ is small.

Fix 3

Regularisation

Keep the features, but penalise large weights. Ridge / L2 regression minimises $\lVert y - \Phi\theta\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\theta\rVert^2$ beyond syllabus, with solution $\theta^* = (\Phi^\top\Phi + \lambda I)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$.

Fix 4

More data

Overfitting is a ratio of capacity to data. Doubling $N$ moves the interpolation threshold ($M+1 = N$) to the right and shifts the whole U-curve's minimum to a higher complexity. Often the cheapest fix, when it is available at all.

Why $\lambda$ removes the wiggles. The violent oscillations of the $k = 30$ fit are produced by enormous coefficients of alternating sign that very nearly cancel on the training points and fail to cancel between them. The penalty $\lambda\lVert\theta\rVert^2$ charges you for exactly that: a coefficient of $10^{7}$ costs $10^{14}\lambda$. So the optimiser will not buy a perfect training fit at that price. Raise $\lambda$ and the wiggles shrink continuously; at $\lambda \to \infty$ all weights go to zero and you underfit completely. So $\lambda$ is a continuous complexity dial, whereas degree is a chunky integer one — which is why in practice people use a high degree and a tuned $\lambda$ rather than hunting for the perfect degree. It also fixes the invertibility problem of §7.1 for free.

Guided problemDiagnose four models from their error numbers

You fit polynomial transforms of four degrees on $N = 10$ training points and measure on 40 held-out points. Which degree do you ship, and what is wrong with each of the others?

$M$train MSEtest MSE
10.1460.339
50.0090.157
90.0000.355
150.0001.272

(These are the real numbers from the widget in §9.)

Step 1 — Read the training column alone. What can you conclude?

Almost nothing. It falls monotonically: 0.146 → 0.009 → 0.000 → 0.000. That is guaranteed by construction, because the degree-15 hypothesis class contains the degree-9 class, which contains degree 5, which contains degree 1. A larger class cannot have a worse best member.

So the training column can never tell you to stop. Any model-selection rule based on training error alone picks the biggest model, every time.

Step 2 — Read the test column

0.339 → 0.157 → 0.355 → 1.272. It falls then rises: the U. Minimum at $M = 5$.

$M = 1$: both errors are high-ish and the gap is modest ($0.339/0.146 \approx 2.3$). Classic underfitting — not enough capacity.

$M = 9$ and $M = 15$: train is exactly zero while test is worse than at $M=1$. The gap is infinite in ratio terms. Textbook overfitting. And note $M = 15$ gives $M + 1 = 16 > 10 = N$, so by §7.1 the solution isn't even unique — a solver detail determines which of infinitely many perfect fits you got.

Step 3 — Is $M=5$ definitely the answer?

It is the best of the four measured, which is not the same as best overall. Two caveats worth stating out loud:

(a) You only sampled four degrees. $M = 3$ or $M = 4$ might do better, and they are simpler — with two models of comparable test error, prefer the simpler one, because its estimate is more stable and it will degrade more gracefully on new data.

(b) You just used the test set to choose $M$. That makes 0.157 an optimistic estimate of the true risk of the selected model — you have leaked the test set into the model-selection decision. Correct practice: choose on a validation set, then report the test number once, at the end, never having looked at it.

Answer & the takeaway

Ship $M = 5$ (or check $M = 3,4$ first and ship the simplest one within noise of the best). $M = 1$ underfits; $M = 9$ and $M = 15$ overfit, with $M = 15$ additionally rank-deficient.

Takeaway: never make a model-selection decision from training error. And keep the set you use to report separate from the set you use to choose.

11. The same story, in a different chapter

Overfitting is not a quirk of polynomial features. It is what happens to any model with enough capacity to memorise, and you have already seen one.

In chapter 6, what happens to a decision tree grown with no depth limit? It keeps splitting until every leaf is pure. With distinct feature vectors it can always get there — in the limit, one leaf per training sample. Training error: zero. Test error: bad, because a leaf built from one sample predicts that sample's label and knows nothing else.

Line the two up:

Polynomial featuresDecision trees
Capacity dialDegree $M$Depth / number of leaves
Memorisation point$M + 1 = N$: the polynomial interpolatesOne sample per leaf: every leaf pure
SymptomViolent wiggles between training pointsSplits on noise; tiny leaves with confident labels
Capacity limiterChoose a lower degree; add $\lambda\lVert\theta\rVert^2$Stopping criteria: max depth, min samples per leaf, min information gain; pruning
Key idea

A tree's stopping criteria are its version of limiting capacity — exactly what choosing a smaller $M$ does for polynomial features, and what $\lambda$ does for ridge. Different algorithms, same dial, same U-curve, same rule: measure on data you did not fit on.

You fit a degree-20 polynomial to 200 points and get train MSE 0.02, test MSE 0.03. Your colleague says "degree 20 is way too complex, you must be overfitting." Who is right?
Why: Overfitting is not a property of the degree; it is a property of the relationship between capacity and data, and it is diagnosed by the train/test gap, not by the parameter count. With $N = 200$ and $M + 1 = 21$ you have ten samples per parameter, and the near-equal errors confirm the model is generalising. The same degree 20 on 10 points would be catastrophic. Always diagnose from the gap, never from the number of features alone.

12. Common mistakes, collected

Common mistake
  1. "$h(x)=b+w_1x+w_2x^2$ is a non-linear model." It is non-linear in $x$ and linear in $\theta$. Only the second fact decides whether linear regression applies.
  2. Forgetting $\phi$ at prediction time. §3.1. The shipped model is the pair $(\phi, \theta)$, and the failure is silent.
  3. Fitting $\phi$'s own parameters on the test set. If $\phi$ standardises using $\mu$ and $s$, those must be computed on the training data only and then reused. Computing them over train+test leaks test information into training.
  4. Reading $x^{(i)}$ as a power. Check for parentheses. In Table 2 both kinds appear in one cell.
  5. Treating engineered features as independent coordinates. $x$ and $x^2$ are perfectly dependent; real data lies on a curve in feature space, and off that curve the model is pure extrapolation.
  6. Choosing the model by training error. It is monotone in capacity. It can only ever tell you to add more.
  7. Assuming more features is never worse. It is never worse on the training set — which is exactly the problem.
  8. Raising raw, unscaled $x$ to high powers. If $x \sim 100$, then $x^6 \sim 10^{12}$; $\Phi^\top\Phi$ becomes numerically hopeless. Standardise before the transform.
Which of these models can not be fitted by ordinary linear regression after a feature transform?
Why: Options A and C are weighted sums of fixed functions of the input, so define $\phi$ to emit those functions and the prediction is $\theta^\top\phi(x)$ — linear in $\theta$, normal equations apply. In option B the parameter $w_2$ sits inside the exponential, so the prediction is not a linear function of $(b, w_1, w_2)$, the objective is not a quadratic in the parameters, and no amount of feature engineering fixes it. You would need iterative non-linear optimisation (see chapter 9), with no guarantee of a global optimum. The rule: the parameters must sit outside, multiplying fixed functions of $x$.

13. Recap

  • Complex data defeats simple models — but the fix can be applied to the data rather than the model.
  • $h(x) = b + w_1x + w_2x^2$ is non-linear in $x$ and linear in the parameters, so ordinary linear regression fits it. Add a column, change nothing else.
  • The transform is part of the model. Apply the same $\phi$ at training and at prediction, or fail silently.
  • With two features you are fitting a plane; the curve you see in 1-D is that plane's height read along the constraint curve $x_2 = x^2$.
  • Polynomial feature transforms generalise this to degree $M$, with a column of ones for the bias. $x^{(i)}$ is an index, $x^i$ is a power.
  • Any function works: $\phi : \R^K \to \R^M$, stacked row-wise into $\Phi \in \R^{N\times M}$ (often just renamed $X$). For degree 3, $\phi(x) = [1, x, x^2, x^3]^\top$.
  • The normal equations are unchanged: $\theta^* = (\Phi^\top\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top y$ — unless $M \ge N$, where $\Phi^\top\Phi$ is singular and infinitely many perfect fits exist.
  • Overfitting: past a point, empirical risk keeps falling while true risk rises. Training error is monotone and cannot warn you. Measure on held-out data.
  • Underfitting is the mirror failure: high error everywhere, rigid model, patterned residuals.
  • Cures: validation sets, cross-validation, regularisation, more data. Trees do the same thing with stopping criteria.
