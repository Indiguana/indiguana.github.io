Part II · Chapter 11

Logistic regression

How to turn a straight line into a classifier that reports how sure it is — and why the sigmoid, not a threshold, is the only function that lets you train the thing.

1. Why a hard decision is not enough

Every classifier you have met so far in this course answers a question of the form: which class does this sample belong to? Decision trees walk you down to a leaf and hand you a label. The course notes put it exactly this way:

Key idea

Previously, we viewed classification as making an explicit decision on whether a given sample $x^{(i)}$ belongs to one of $K$ classes. But making a discrete decision on the class of $x^{(i)}$ doesn't tell us how strongly we believe $x^{(i)}$ belongs to that class.

That observation is what pushes us toward producing a probability distribution over classes at prediction time.

Notation first, as always: $x^{(i)}$ means the $i$-th sample in the dataset. The parenthesised superscript is an index, not an exponent. $x^{(3)}$ is the third data point; $x^3$ is $x$ cubed. The course notes flag this too, and this chapter will put both kinds of superscript on the same page, so keep checking for the parentheses.

1.1 The concrete thing that goes wrong

Suppose a model screens chest X-rays and outputs "tumour" or "no tumour". Two patients come back "tumour". One of them sat squarely in the middle of a cloud of obviously-cancerous scans. The other sat one pixel over the line, in a region where half the training scans went each way.

The model gave you the same word for both. You cannot:

  • Triage. If you have capacity to biopsy 20 patients today and 100 came back positive, which 20?
  • Defer. You would like the model to say "I genuinely don't know, send this to a human." A label has no vocabulary for that.
  • Re-tune the threshold. A false negative here costs a life; a false positive costs a biopsy. Those are not equally bad (this is the asymmetric-loss idea from chapter 5). To trade one against the other you need a dial, and a bare label has no dial.
  • Combine models. Two labels cannot be averaged sensibly. Two probabilities can.
Analogy

A weather forecaster who only ever says "rain" or "no rain" is much less useful than one who says "70% chance of rain" — even though on any single day they say the same thing. The percentage lets you decide whether to carry an umbrella, cancel the picnic, or close the airport, all from one forecast. The bare word forces the forecaster to make your decision for you, using their idea of what rain costs you.

Where the analogy breaks: a forecaster's 70% is calibrated — over many such days it really does rain about 70% of the time. A model's output number is only a probability if you check that it behaves like one. We come back to this in the common mistakes.

1.2 Why linear, specifically

The notes make a second observation right after the first, and it is the reason this chapter sits where it does in the course:

Key idea

Some classification models, such as decision trees, aren't linear functions of their input $x^{(i)}$ at all. They perform some non-linear function in order to decide their classes.

Logistic regression models are an intuitive extension of linear regression that let us perform classification while still building on a linear model.

Unpack "a decision tree isn't a linear function of its input". A tree that asks "is $x_1 > 3$?" then "is $x_2 > 7$?" carves feature space into axis-aligned boxes and assigns a label to each box. Its output as a function of $x$ is a staircase: piecewise constant, jumping at split points. There are no coefficients $w_j$ to read. You cannot say "increasing $x_1$ by one unit increases the score by $w_1$", because the tree's score doesn't move at all until you cross a split, and then it jumps.

Linear models give up that flexibility and get three things in return: interpretable coefficients (one number per feature, with a sign and a magnitude), easy optimisation (we will get a convex objective), and simplicity (a handful of parameters, so less to overfit — the lesson of chapter 10). Logistic regression is the model that keeps all three and outputs probabilities.

The line crosses $\hat y = 0.5$ at $x = 0.967$ — right in the overlap, which is encouraging. But look at the ends: at $x = -1.4$ the model predicts $\hat y = -0.189$, and at $x = 3.3$ it predicts $\hat y = 1.180$. Both are outside $\{0,1\}$ and outside $[0,1]$.

What did we learn, and what went wrong? Quoting the notes: the model "learns some relationship between the input $x$ and the class $y$, where the class will increase if the input increases." The slope is positive, which is correct — larger $x$ really does mean more likely class 1. So the fit is not useless.

But: "this doesn't necessarily constrain the output of a model to the appropriate class that we would like, either a 0 or a 1." A linear function is unbounded. Push $x$ far enough left and you get $-0.4$; far enough right and you get $1.7$. On our data the extremes are $-0.189$ and $1.180$, and with a point at $x = 10$ the fit predicts $1.433$. "$\hat y = 1.433$" is not a class. It is not a probability either. It is a number with no interpretation at all.

The failure mode the notes skip, and you should see

There is a second, sneakier problem with using squared loss for classification, and it is worth a figure of its own because it explains a phenomenon you will otherwise find baffling.

Squared loss punishes the model for being far from the target number. A point sitting at $x = 10$ with label $1$ is, from a classification point of view, the easiest point in the world: it is miles inside class-1 territory and any sane boundary gets it right. But least squares looks at it and sees $\hat y = 0.2184 + 0.2913(10) = 3.13$, which is $2.13$ away from the target $1$, for a squared penalty of $4.5$ — larger than the penalty from all twelve real points combined. So the fit contorts itself to bring that one far-away point down toward $1$, and the only way to do that is to flatten the slope.

Figure B — one far-away, correctly-labelled point moves the boundary
Watch the crossing point. Before: $\hat y = 0.2184 + 0.2913x$, boundary at $x = 0.967$, one point misclassified (the class-0 point at $x = 1.2$). After adding $(x{=}10,\ y{=}1)$: $\hat y = 0.3601 + 0.1073x$, boundary at $x = 1.303$, and now two points are wrong — the class-1 points at $x = 1.0$ and $x = 1.3$. Intuition

Squared loss has unbounded appetite. There is no such thing as "right enough" — a prediction of $3.13$ when the target is $1$ is scored as a catastrophe, even though as a classification it is perfect. So a handful of easy, extreme points can outvote all your hard, informative points near the boundary.

The loss we build later in this chapter does not have this problem. Its per-point pressure saturates: once a point is confidently correct, its gradient contribution goes to zero and it stops voting. When I refit the logistic model of §7 with and without that same point at $x = 10$, the fitted parameters agree to four decimal places: $w = 5.5119$, $b = -5.8044$ both times. Identical. The far-away point contributes nothing because the model already assigns it probability $0.99999$ and there is nothing left to gain.

2.3 Stage 3 — threshold the line (Figure 7)

The outputs are invalid, so clamp them. Pick the natural cut at $0.5$: call it class $0$ if the linear regression is below $0.5$, class $1$ otherwise. The notes write this as a new model

$$ \hat{y} = g_{\text{threshold}}(wx + b), \qquad g_{\text{threshold}}(z) = \begin{cases} 0 & z < 0.5\\ 1 & z \ge 0.5\end{cases} $$

(As written in the notes the threshold is applied at the value $0.5$ of the linear output; equivalently you can absorb the $0.5$ into $b$ and threshold at $0$. Nothing below depends on which convention you pick.)

Figure 7 (reconstructed) — $\D$ trained on a thresholding model
Now every output is a legal class. But the model's belief goes from "definitely 0" to "definitely 1" across an infinitesimal sliver of the $x$-axis, and its slope is exactly zero everywhere else.

The notes name both problems. Take them in order, because the second one is the argument that actually forces the design.

Problem (a) — the model is absurdly certain

In the notes' words: "The model is extremely certain about its predictions! It transitions rapidly from one class to the other right around input values $x = 1$. However, looking at the overlap in our data, we probably want our model to state that for $x = 1$, $P(Y = 1 \mid x) \approx 0.5$."

That is precisely the overlap we read off the table in §2.1. At $x = 1.0$ we have a class-1 point; at $x = 1.2$ a class-0 point. The honest answer at $x = 1$ is "roughly a coin flip." The threshold model instead reports certainty $1$ for class 0 at $x = 0.9666$ and certainty $1$ for class 1 at $x = 0.9668$. It has thrown away every bit of information about where the sample sits relative to the boundary — which is exactly the information §1 said we wanted.

Problem (b) — there is nothing for gradient descent to descend

The notes: "it has a derivative of zero essentially everywhere, making it very problematic to optimize over." This sentence is the hinge of the whole chapter, so let us make it completely precise.

Proof

Claim. Gradient descent cannot train $\hat y = g_{\text{threshold}}(wx+b)$.

Recall from chapter 9 the update rule:

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla_\theta J(\theta) $$

where $J(\theta)$ is the training objective, $\alpha > 0$ is the learning rate, and $\nabla_\theta J$ is the gradient — the vector of partial derivatives $\partial J / \partial \theta_j$. The rule has exactly one input: the gradient. If the gradient is the zero vector, the update does nothing, whatever $\alpha$ is.

Now compute that gradient. Whatever per-sample loss $\ell$ you use, the chain rule gives

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial w} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \hat y}}_{\text{depends on the loss}} \cdot \underbrace{g_{\text{threshold}}'(z)}_{\text{the model}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}}_{=\,x}, \qquad z = wx+b. $$

The middle factor is the derivative of a step function. A step function is constant on $z < 0.5$ and constant on $z > 0.5$, so $g_{\text{threshold}}'(z) = 0$ at every $z \ne 0.5$. At $z = 0.5$ exactly, the function jumps, and the derivative does not exist (the left and right difference quotients blow up to $\pm\infty$).

So the middle factor is $0$ for almost every input, and undefined on a set of measure zero. Multiplying anything by that middle factor gives $0$. Therefore $\nabla_\theta J = \mathbf{0}$ almost everywhere, and

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \cdot \mathbf{0} = \theta. $$

Gradient descent takes a step of length zero, forever. $\blacksquare$

Here is the same fact stated in terms of the landscape rather than the formula, because that is how to remember it. With a threshold model, changing $w$ by a tiny amount either changes no labels at all (the usual case) or flips one point's label (when the boundary sweeps past it). So the training loss, as a function of $\theta$, is a staircase: flat plateaus with sudden drops. Gradient descent on a staircase is a blindfolded person on a perfectly flat floor being told to walk downhill. The floor is lower somewhere — but not here, and the slope gives no hint which way to go.

Key idea

The requirement that kills the threshold is not "outputs must be probabilities". It is "the loss must have a usable slope with respect to the parameters." Everything in modern machine learning that is trained by gradient descent obeys this constraint, and it is the reason models are built out of smooth pieces. The sigmoid is not chosen because it is pretty. It is chosen because it is differentiable and non-flat.

2.4 Stage 4 — replace the step with a smooth S (Figure 8)

The notes draw the conclusion cleanly: to make a valid classifier we applied a function $g$ to the linear combination $wx + b$. Keep that structure, but choose a $g$ that "smoothly maps all real input values $(-\infty, \infty)$ to the range $(0,1)$" — i.e. to a probability for binary classification. The standard choice is a sigmoid ("s-shaped") function, specifically the logistic function, which is so common that "the sigmoid" almost always means this one, written $\sigma$:

Definition $$ g_{\text{logistic}}(z) \;=\; \sigma(z) \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} $$

$z \in \R$ is any real number (in our model it will be $wx+b$), $e \approx 2.71828$ is Euler's number, and $\sigma(z)$ is the output, which we will prove lies strictly between $0$ and $1$.

The notes then say: "Prove to yourself that this function outputs values in $[0,1]$ and outputs $0.5$ when the input is $0$." That is an exercise left to the reader, so let us actually do it.

3. The logistic function, proved and differentiated

Proof

Claim 1: $0 < \sigma(z) < 1$ for every real $z$.

Upper bound. The exponential is strictly positive: $e^{-z} > 0$ for every real $z$ (it never reaches zero, it only approaches it). Therefore

$$ 1 + e^{-z} \;>\; 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \;<\; \frac{1}{1} = 1. $$

The implication is legal because $t \mapsto 1/t$ is strictly decreasing on $t > 0$, and both $1+e^{-z}$ and $1$ are positive.

Lower bound. For any fixed real $z$, $e^{-z}$ is a finite positive number, so $1 + e^{-z}$ is finite and positive, so its reciprocal is positive:

$$ \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \;>\; 0. $$

Together: $\sigma(z) \in (0,1)$ for all $z \in \R$. $\blacksquare$

Claim 2: $\sigma(0) = 0.5$. Substitute $z = 0$. Then $e^{-0} = e^{0} = 1$, so

$$ \sigma(0) = \frac{1}{1+1} = \frac{1}{2}. \qquad \blacksquare $$

Claim 3: the asymptotes. As $z \to +\infty$, the exponent $-z \to -\infty$, so $e^{-z} \to 0$, so the denominator $\to 1$ and

$$ \lim_{z\to+\infty}\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+0} = 1. $$

As $z \to -\infty$, the exponent $-z \to +\infty$, so $e^{-z} \to +\infty$, the denominator grows without bound, and

$$ \lim_{z\to-\infty}\sigma(z) = 0. \qquad \blacksquare $$
Common mistake

The notes write "outputs values in $[0,1]$" with closed brackets, which is the usual informal shorthand. Claim 1 shows the range is actually the open interval $(0,1)$: $\sigma$ gets arbitrarily close to $0$ and $1$ but never equals either. That matters twice later — once because it means logistic regression can never be absolutely certain (a feature, not a bug), and once because $\log \hat y$ is therefore always finite, which is what keeps the loss in §6 from returning $\infty$ on real data.

3.1 The derivative — the identity that makes the model trainable

Stage 3 died because its derivative was zero. So the very first thing to ask of $\sigma$ is: what is $\sigma'$? The answer is startlingly tidy, and every gradient in the rest of this chapter rests on it.

Proof

Claim: $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,\bigl(1 - \sigma(z)\bigr)$.

Step 1 — rewrite as a power. $\sigma(z) = \bigl(1 + e^{-z}\bigr)^{-1}$.

Step 2 — chain rule. With $u(z) = 1 + e^{-z}$ we have $\sigma = u^{-1}$, so $\dfrac{d\sigma}{dz} = -u^{-2}\dfrac{du}{dz}$. This is legal because $u(z) > 0$ everywhere, so $u^{-1}$ is differentiable.

Step 3 — the inner derivative. $\dfrac{du}{dz} = \dfrac{d}{dz}\bigl(1 + e^{-z}\bigr) = 0 + e^{-z}\cdot(-1) = -e^{-z}$, using $\frac{d}{dz}e^{cz} = ce^{cz}$ with $c=-1$.

Step 4 — substitute.

$$ \sigma'(z) \;=\; -\bigl(1+e^{-z}\bigr)^{-2}\cdot\bigl(-e^{-z}\bigr) \;=\; \frac{e^{-z}}{\bigl(1+e^{-z}\bigr)^{2}}. $$

Step 5 — split the fraction. This is the only clever step. Write the single fraction as a product of two:

$$ \frac{e^{-z}}{\bigl(1+e^{-z}\bigr)^{2}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}}_{=\;\sigma(z)}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}}_{=\;?} $$

Step 6 — identify the second factor. Add and subtract $1$ in the numerator:

$$ \frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} \;=\; \frac{\bigl(1+e^{-z}\bigr) - 1}{1+e^{-z}} \;=\; \frac{1+e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}} - \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \;=\; 1 - \sigma(z). $$

Conclusion. $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)$. $\blacksquare$

Three consequences, each of which you will use:

  1. $\sigma' > 0$ everywhere. Since $\sigma \in (0,1)$, both $\sigma$ and $1-\sigma$ are strictly positive, so their product is strictly positive. Hence $\sigma$ is strictly increasing — which is what lets us invert the inequality $\sigma(z) \ge 0.5 \iff z \ge 0$ in §5.
  2. The derivative is largest at the boundary. $\sigma(1-\sigma)$ is a downward parabola in $\sigma$, maximised at $\sigma = 1/2$, i.e. at $z = 0$, where $\sigma'(0) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = 0.25$. The model is most sensitive exactly where it is most uncertain. That is the opposite of the threshold, whose derivative was zero everywhere except at the boundary.
  3. The derivative is expressed in terms of the function's own output. You never need to recompute $e^{-z}$: if you already have $\hat y = \sigma(z)$, then $\sigma'(z) = \hat y (1 - \hat y)$, one multiply. This is why neural-network backpropagation is cheap.

3.2 One more identity: $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$

Proof $$ \sigma(-z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-(-z)}} = \frac{1}{1+e^{z}}. $$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $e^{-z}$ (legal: $e^{-z} \ne 0$):

$$ \frac{1}{1+e^{z}} \cdot \frac{e^{-z}}{e^{-z}} = \frac{e^{-z}}{e^{-z} + e^{z}e^{-z}} = \frac{e^{-z}}{e^{-z} + 1} = 1 - \sigma(z), $$

where the last equality is Step 6 of the previous proof. $\blacksquare$

In words: the sigmoid is antisymmetric about the point $(0, 0.5)$. Rotating the curve $180°$ about that point maps it onto itself. Practically, this says the model treats the two classes symmetrically — being $2$ units to the right of the boundary makes you class 1 with probability $0.8808$, and being $2$ units to the left makes you class 0 with exactly the same probability $0.8808$.

4. The logistic regression model (Figure 9)

Now assemble it. Apply $\sigma$ to the linear transformation of the input, exactly as we applied $g_{\text{threshold}}$ before:

Definition

One-dimensional input ($x \in \R$):

$$ h(x) = \hat y = \sigma(wx + b) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(wx+b)}} $$

$M$-dimensional input ($x \in \R^M$, with the bias term folded in as a constant feature):

$$ h(x) = \hat y = \sigma(\theta^\top x) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\theta^\top x}} $$

Every symbol: $x \in \R^M$ is one sample's feature vector, $\theta \in \R^M$ is the parameter vector, $\theta^\top x = \sum_{j=1}^{M}\theta_j x_j$ is the dot product (a single real number, often called the logit or score), $\sigma$ is the logistic function, and $h$ is the hypothesis function.

What "the bias term is included in this $x$" means. The notes fold $b$ into $\theta$ by the trick you already used in chapter 10: prepend a constant $1$ to every feature vector. If the raw input is $\tilde x = (x_1,\dots,x_{M-1})$, set $x = (1, x_1, \dots, x_{M-1})$ and $\theta = (\theta_0, \theta_1, \dots, \theta_{M-1})$. Then

$$ \theta^\top x = \theta_0 \cdot 1 + \theta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \theta_{M-1}x_{M-1} $$

and $\theta_0$ plays the role of $b$. One notation, no special cases. From here on, "$\theta^\top x$" always includes the bias.

4.1 Reading the output as a probability

This is the payoff of §1. Quoting the notes: "Because the output of the model $h(x)$ is a value in $(0,1)$, we can re-think this value as a probability where $h(x) = P(Y = 1 \mid x)$."

Key idea $$ h(x) = P(Y = 1 \mid x), \qquad\text{and therefore}\qquad P(Y = 0 \mid x) = 1 - h(x). $$

Read $P(Y=1 \mid x)$ as: "the probability that this sample's label is 1, given that we observed features $x$." The second equation is not an extra assumption — with only two classes, the probabilities must sum to $1$, so $P(Y=0\mid x)$ is forced.

So the model does not output a class. It outputs a full probability distribution over the two classes, $\bigl(1-h(x),\; h(x)\bigr)$. Every complaint in §1.1 is now answerable: you can rank patients by $h(x)$, you can flag $h(x) \in (0.4, 0.6)$ for human review, you can move the operating threshold up or down to trade false positives against false negatives, and you can average two models' probabilities.

Note the pleasing symmetry this gives with §3.2: since $P(Y=0\mid x) = 1 - \sigma(\theta^\top x) = \sigma(-\theta^\top x)$, the "probability of class 0" is the same model with the sign of $\theta$ flipped. Class 0 and class 1 are on an equal footing; nothing about the model prefers one.

Intuition

Everything in a logistic regression happens in two separate steps, and keeping them separate makes the model much easier to think about.

Step 1 (linear, learnable): $z = \theta^\top x$ collapses the whole feature vector down to one number — a score on a line. All the learning lives here.

Step 2 (fixed, non-linear): $\sigma$ squashes that score into a probability. There are no parameters in $\sigma$; it is a fixed piece of plumbing.

So a logistic regression is a linear model wearing a probability-shaped hat. Honest caveat on the analogy: the hat is not merely cosmetic, because $\sigma$ changes what the loss surface looks like and therefore what fit you get. But it never changes which functions the model can express as a decision rule — as §5 proves.

Our fitted model is $h(x) = \sigma(5.5119x - 5.8044)$. What is $P(Y = 0 \mid x = 1.053)$?
Why: $x=1.053$ makes the logit $z = 5.5119(1.053) - 5.8044 \approx 0$, and $\sigma(0)=0.5$, so $h(x)=0.5$ and $P(Y=0\mid x) = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5$. The decision boundary is exactly the set of points where the model is maximally unsure — not where it is certain. It is also where $\sigma' $ is largest ($0.25$), i.e. where the model is most responsive to a parameter change.

5. Why this is still a linear classifier

Here is the apparent paradox. We just wrapped the linear score in $\sigma$, which is emphatically non-linear — it curves, it saturates, it has an inflection point. Yet the notes insist logistic regression is a linear model for classification. Both things are true, and the reason is worth a proof.

Proof

Claim. The rule "predict class 1 when $h(x) \ge 0.5$" is identical to the rule "predict class 1 when $\theta^\top x \ge 0$".

Start from the prediction rule and substitute the model:

$$ h(x) \ge 0.5 \iff \sigma(\theta^\top x) \ge 0.5 . $$

By Claim 2 of §3, $0.5 = \sigma(0)$, so this is

$$ \sigma(\theta^\top x) \ge \sigma(0). $$

By consequence 1 of §3.1, $\sigma' (z) = \sigma(1-\sigma) > 0$ for all $z$, so $\sigma$ is strictly increasing, hence injective, hence it has a strictly increasing inverse. A strictly increasing function preserves $\ge$ in both directions:

$$ \sigma(a) \ge \sigma(b) \iff a \ge b. $$

Apply it with $a = \theta^\top x$ and $b = 0$:

$$ \boxed{\;h(x) \ge 0.5 \iff \theta^\top x \ge 0\;} $$

The set $\{x : \theta^\top x = 0\}$ is, by definition, a hyperplane through the origin of the augmented feature space — a point in 1-D, a line in 2-D, a plane in 3-D. The decision boundary is that hyperplane. $\blacksquare$

Concretely, in 1-D with $z = wx+b$, the boundary is the single point $x = -b/w$. In 2-D with $z = \theta_0 + \theta_1x_1 + \theta_2x_2$, the boundary is the straight line $\theta_0 + \theta_1x_1 + \theta_2x_2 = 0$.

Key idea

$\sigma$ is non-linear, but it is a monotone non-linearity applied after the linear part. Monotone functions cannot re-order their inputs, so they cannot change which side of the boundary a point falls on. All $\sigma$ does is relabel the score axis — it turns "score" into "probability" without moving anybody across the line.

That is why logistic regression inherits exactly the same expressive limits as a linear classifier: it cannot learn XOR, and it cannot carve out a circle. If you need curved boundaries, you do what chapter 10 taught you — engineer features. Feed it $x_1^2, x_2^2, x_1x_2$ and the boundary becomes a conic section in the original space while staying a hyperplane in the engineered space.

5.1 What $\|\theta\|$ does versus what the direction of $\theta$ does

Split the parameter vector into the bias and the rest: $\theta = (\theta_0, w)$ with $w = (\theta_1,\dots,\theta_{M-1})$ acting on the real features $\tilde x$. Then $z = w^\top \tilde x + \theta_0$, and from geometry, the signed distance from a point $\tilde x$ to the boundary hyperplane is

$$ d(\tilde x) = \frac{w^\top \tilde x + \theta_0}{\|w\|} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z = \|w\|\,d(\tilde x). $$

Substituting into the model:

$$ h(x) = \sigma\bigl(\|w\|\; d(\tilde x)\bigr). $$

Now everything separates cleanly:

Direction of $w$

Where the line is, and which side is which

The unit vector $w/\|w\|$ is the normal to the boundary — it sets the orientation of the hyperplane and points toward the class-1 side. The bias $\theta_0$ slides the boundary along that normal without rotating it. Change the direction and you rotate the decision line.

Magnitude $\|w\|$

How fast belief changes as you cross

$\|w\|$ multiplies the distance before it enters $\sigma$, so it controls the sharpness of the transition — the width of the fuzzy band. Doubling $\|w\|$ halves the band. As $\|w\| \to \infty$ the model degenerates back into the threshold of stage 3; as $\|w\| \to 0$ it says $0.5$ everywhere. The boundary itself does not move.

The last italicised sentence is the one students trip over, so let us verify it: if you replace $\theta$ by $c\theta$ for any $c > 0$, the boundary set is $\{x : (c\theta)^\top x = 0\} = \{x : c\,(\theta^\top x) = 0\} = \{x: \theta^\top x = 0\}$ — the same set. Scaling $\theta$ changes every probability but not a single predicted label.

The colour is $h(x) = P(Y=1\mid x)$, running from the class-0 colour to the class-1 colour. The white-ish ridge is the $h = 0.5$ contour: the decision boundary. The arrow is the direction of $w$. $$ (2,1):\ z = 2 \Rightarrow h = \sigma(2) = 0.8808, \qquad (1,0.5):\ z = -2 \Rightarrow h = \sigma(-2) = 0.1192. $$

Takeaway. $\|\theta\|$ is a confidence dial that the decision rule cannot see. This has a sharp practical consequence: if your training data happens to be perfectly linearly separable, the likelihood can always be improved by scaling $\theta$ up, so the optimiser drives $\|\theta\| \to \infty$ and never converges. beyond syllabus The fix is regularisation — add a penalty $\lambda\|\theta\|^2$ to the objective, which gives $\|\theta\|$ a reason to stay finite. Our dataset has overlapping classes, so this does not bite here; it is the first thing to check when a logistic regression "won't converge".

6. Log-odds: why the coefficients mean something

We have a probability. Can we get back to the linear part? Yes, and the inverse has a name you will meet constantly.

Definition

For a probability $p \in (0,1)$, the odds are $\dfrac{p}{1-p}$ — "how many times more likely the event is than its complement". A probability of $0.75$ is odds of $3$, i.e. 3-to-1 on. The log-odds or logit is $\log\dfrac{p}{1-p}$, which maps $(0,1)$ onto all of $\R$.

Proof

Claim: $\displaystyle \log\frac{h(x)}{1-h(x)} = \theta^\top x$. Write $z = \theta^\top x$.

Numerator. $h = \sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$.

Denominator. From Step 6 of §3.1, $1 - \sigma(z) = \dfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}$.

Divide. The common denominator cancels:

$$ \frac{h}{1-h} \;=\; \frac{\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-z}}}{\dfrac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}} \;=\; \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\cdot\frac{1+e^{-z}}{e^{-z}} \;=\; \frac{1}{e^{-z}} \;=\; e^{z}. $$

Take logs. $\log$ is the inverse of $\exp$, so

$$ \log\frac{h(x)}{1-h(x)} = \log e^{z} = z = \theta^\top x. \qquad \blacksquare $$
Key idea

Logistic regression is ordinary linear regression performed on the log-odds scale. The quantity being modelled linearly is not the probability — it is $\log\frac{p}{1-p}$, which lives on all of $\R$ and so is a sensible thing for an unbounded linear function to predict. The sigmoid is nothing but the inverse of the logit, applied to convert back.

This is what makes the coefficients interpretable. Hold every feature fixed except $x_j$, and increase $x_j$ by one unit. Then $z$ increases by exactly $\theta_j$, so

$$ \frac{\text{new odds}}{\text{old odds}} = \frac{e^{z + \theta_j}}{e^{z}} = e^{\theta_j}. $$
Example

You fit a model predicting whether a student passes a course. The coefficient on hours studied per week comes out as $\theta_j = 0.7$.

Interpretation: each extra hour per week multiplies the odds of passing by $e^{0.7} = 2.0138$ — it roughly doubles the odds. Not the probability: the odds.

Worked numbers. Suppose a particular student currently has $P(\text{pass}) = 0.4$.

  • Current odds: $\dfrac{0.4}{1-0.4} = \dfrac{0.4}{0.6} = 0.6667$.
  • Add one study hour: odds $\times\, 2.0138 = 0.6667 \times 2.0138 = 1.3425$.
  • Convert back to a probability with $p = \dfrac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}} = \dfrac{1.3425}{2.3425} = 0.5731$.

So the probability went from $0.400$ to $0.573$, a jump of $17.3$ percentage points. Now do the same hour for a student already at $P = 0.95$: odds $= 19$, new odds $= 38.26$, new $p = 38.26/39.26 = 0.9745$ — a jump of only $2.5$ points.

The moral: the effect on the odds is a constant multiplier, the same for everybody. The effect on the probability depends on where you started, and is largest near $p = 0.5$. That is the sigmoid's saturation, restated. It is also why reporting logistic coefficients as "a one-hour increase raises the pass rate by 17 points" is wrong — it is only true for students near $40\%$.

A logistic model of loan default has coefficient $\theta_j = -0.3$ on years of credit history. Which statement is correct?
Why: the linear part models the log-odds, so adding $1$ to $x_j$ adds $\theta_j$ to the log-odds and therefore multiplies the odds by $e^{\theta_j} = e^{-0.3} = 0.7408$. Option A confuses odds with probability and also treats a multiplicative effect as additive. Option C multiplies the wrong quantity: odds and probability only agree when $p$ is tiny (then $p/(1-p)\approx p$), so C is a decent approximation for rare events and wrong otherwise. Option D is backwards — the sign is the most interpretable part of the whole model: negative coefficient, feature pushes toward class 0.

7.1 Why not squared loss?

The obvious move is to reuse the loss from chapter 8:

$$ J_{\text{sq}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(y^{(i)} - \sigma\bigl(\theta^\top x^{(i)}\bigr)\Bigr)^{2}. $$

It is not absurd — it is bounded, it is differentiable, it is minimised when $\hat y = y$. But it fails on two counts, and both matter.

Failure 1: it is not convex in $\theta$

For linear regression, squared loss gave a bowl: one global minimum, no local traps, and a closed-form solution. Wrap the prediction in $\sigma$ and the bowl buckles. Here is the smallest possible demonstration, which you can check by hand.

Proof

Take a dataset of one point, $x = 1$ (no bias), $y = 0$. Then $\hat y = \sigma(w)$ and

$$ J(w) = \bigl(0 - \sigma(w)\bigr)^2 = \sigma(w)^2 . $$

Differentiate, using $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$ from §3.1:

$$ J'(w) = 2\sigma\,\sigma' = 2\sigma^{2}(1-\sigma). $$

Differentiate again — product rule on $\sigma^2$ and $(1-\sigma)$, then factor out $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$:

$$ J''(w) = 2\Bigl[2\sigma\sigma'(1-\sigma) - \sigma^{2}\sigma'\Bigr] = 2\sigma'\sigma\Bigl[2(1-\sigma) - \sigma\Bigr] = 2\sigma'\sigma\,(2 - 3\sigma). $$

Since $\sigma' > 0$ and $\sigma > 0$, the sign of $J''$ is the sign of $(2 - 3\sigma)$. So

  • $\sigma < 2/3$ (that is, $w < \log 2 \approx 0.693$): $J'' > 0$, convex;
  • $\sigma > 2/3$ (that is, $w > \log 2$): $J'' < 0$, concave.

Numerically: $J''(0) = +0.125$ and $J''(2) = -0.1188$. A convex function has $J'' \ge 0$ everywhere, so $J_{\text{sq}}$ is not convex. $\blacksquare$

With many points and many parameters this produces genuine local minima and flat plateaus, so gradient descent's answer depends on where you started. That is a bad property for a model whose selling point is simplicity.

Failure 2: the gradient vanishes exactly when the model is most wrong

Differentiate one term of $J_{\text{sq}}$ with respect to $\theta$, chain rule through $\sigma$:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\bigl(y - \sigma(z)\bigr)^2 = -2\bigl(y - \sigma(z)\bigr)\,\underbrace{\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr)}_{\sigma'(z)}\,x . $$

Now imagine the worst case: the true label is $y = 1$ and the model says $\hat y = \sigma(z) = 0.001$. It is as wrong as it is possible to be. The error factor $(y - \hat y) = 0.999$ is large — good. But the $\sigma'$ factor is $0.001 \times 0.999 = 0.000999$. Their product is $\approx 0.000998$. The gradient is a thousandth of the size it would be for a point sitting right at the boundary ($0.25$ there). The model is confidently wrong and the optimiser barely notices.

This is the pathology of stage 3 creeping back in through the side door: saturation makes $\sigma'$ small, and squared loss multiplies by $\sigma'$. We need a loss whose derivative cancels that factor.

7.2 The cross-entropy (negative log-likelihood) loss

Definition

For one sample with true label $y \in \{0,1\}$ and predicted probability $\hat y = h(x) \in (0,1)$, the binary cross-entropy loss is

$$ \ell(y,\hat y) \;=\; -\Bigl[\,y\log \hat y \;+\; (1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\,\Bigr] $$

and the training objective averages it over the dataset:

$$ J(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} -\Bigl[\,y^{(i)}\log \sigma\bigl(\theta^\top x^{(i)}\bigr) + \bigl(1-y^{(i)}\bigr)\log\bigl(1 - \sigma(\theta^\top x^{(i)})\bigr)\Bigr]. $$

Read the two branches. $y$ is either $0$ or $1$, so one of the two terms is always multiplied by zero and disappears. The formula is a switch written without an if:

  • If $y = 1$: the second term dies ($1-y = 0$) and $\ell = -\log\hat y$. You are charged for the log of the probability you assigned to the right answer. Say $\hat y = 0.9$ and you pay $0.105$; say $\hat y = 0.5$ and you pay $0.693$; say $\hat y = 0.01$ and you pay $4.605$.
  • If $y = 0$: the first term dies and $\ell = -\log(1-\hat y)$. Same charge, applied to $1 - \hat y = P(Y=0\mid x)$, which is again the probability you assigned to the right answer.

Both branches are the same sentence: your loss is $-\log(\text{the probability you gave the true label})$.

Intuition

Where does $-\log$ come from? Two equivalent stories.

Likelihood. The model claims the label is a Bernoulli draw: $P(y \mid x) = \hat y^{\,y}(1-\hat y)^{1-y}$ (check both cases: $y=1$ gives $\hat y$, $y=0$ gives $1-\hat y$). The probability of the whole dataset, assuming samples are independent, is the product $\prod_i P(y^{(i)}\mid x^{(i)})$. Maximising a product of tiny numbers is numerically awful, so take logs — which turns the product into a sum and is legal because $\log$ is increasing, so it does not move the maximiser. Then flip the sign to turn "maximise" into "minimise". You get exactly $J(\theta)$. Cross-entropy is negative log-likelihood; fitting a logistic regression is maximum likelihood estimation.

Information. From chapter 7, $-\log p$ is the number of nats of surprise in an event you assigned probability $p$. Cross-entropy loss charges you your surprise at the truth. A model that is confidently wrong is infinitely surprised, and pays accordingly.

Figure D — the cost of a prediction, for each true label
Both curves pass through $(0.5,\ 0.693)$ — the cost of shrugging. Both reach $0$ when you put full probability on the truth. And both go to $+\infty$ as you put probability $\to 0$ on the truth: $-\log(0.1) = 2.303$, $-\log(0.01) = 4.605$, $-\log(0.001)=6.908$. The explosion is the design. Squared loss caps a wrong answer's cost at $1$; cross-entropy makes it unbounded, so the optimiser can never make peace with a confidently wrong prediction. Compare the two curves' slopes near the bad end, too: squared loss flattens out there, cross-entropy gets steeper.

7.3 Next step: training it — the gradient

To run gradient descent we need $\nabla_\theta J$. Derive it for a single sample first; the average comes along for free by linearity of differentiation.

Proof

Fix one sample $(x, y)$ and write $z = \theta^\top x$, $\hat y = \sigma(z)$. The per-sample loss is

$$ \ell = -\bigl[\,y\log\sigma(z) + (1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z))\,\bigr]. $$

We want $\partial\ell/\partial\theta$. Chain through $z$:

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial \theta}. $$

Line 1 — differentiate $\ell$ with respect to $z$. Use $\frac{d}{dz}\log u = \frac{1}{u}\frac{du}{dz}$ on each term, with $u = \sigma$ in the first and $u = 1-\sigma$ in the second. Note $\frac{d}{dz}(1-\sigma) = -\sigma'$:

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = -\left[\, y\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma}\cdot\sigma' \;+\; (1-y)\cdot\frac{1}{1-\sigma}\cdot(-\sigma')\,\right]. $$

Line 2 — pull out the common $\sigma'$.

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = -\,\sigma' \left[\, \frac{y}{\sigma} - \frac{1-y}{1-\sigma} \,\right]. $$

Line 3 — substitute the identity $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$. This is the step the whole chapter has been building toward:

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = -\,\sigma(1-\sigma) \left[\, \frac{y}{\sigma} - \frac{1-y}{1-\sigma} \,\right]. $$

Line 4 — distribute, and watch the denominators cancel. The first product cancels $\sigma$; the second cancels $(1-\sigma)$:

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = -\Bigl[\, y(1-\sigma) \;-\; (1-y)\sigma \,\Bigr]. $$

Line 5 — expand the bracket.

$$ y(1-\sigma) - (1-y)\sigma = y - y\sigma - \sigma + y\sigma = y - \sigma. $$

The two $y\sigma$ terms annihilate. Therefore

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial z} = -(y - \sigma) = \sigma(z) - y = \hat y - y . $$

Line 6 — the other factor. $z = \theta^\top x = \sum_j \theta_j x_j$, so $\partial z/\partial\theta_j = x_j$, i.e. $\partial z/\partial\theta = x$.

Line 7 — multiply, and average over the dataset.

$$ \frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta} = \bigl(\sigma(\theta^\top x) - y\bigr)\,x, \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\nabla_\theta J = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(\sigma\bigl(\theta^\top x^{(i)}\bigr) - y^{(i)}\Bigr)\,x^{(i)}\;} $$

$\blacksquare$

Key idea

Look at how clean that is. Every $\log$, every $e$, every $\sigma'$ has cancelled. What is left is

$$ \nabla_\theta J = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (\text{prediction}_i - \text{truth}_i)\cdot x^{(i)} $$

— literally "average the feature vectors, weighted by how wrong you were on each." Compare it with the linear-regression gradient from chapter 8:

$$ \nabla_\theta J_{\text{lin}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \bigl(\theta^\top x^{(i)} - y^{(i)}\bigr)x^{(i)} \qquad\text{vs}\qquad \nabla_\theta J_{\text{log}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \bigl(\sigma(\theta^\top x^{(i)}) - y^{(i)}\bigr)x^{(i)} $$

Identical form. The only difference is that the prediction passes through $\sigma$. This is not a coincidence — it is a general property of the exponential family, of which both models are members. beyond syllabus

And note what this gradient does not have: the $\sigma'$ factor that crippled squared loss. Consider again the confidently-wrong point ($y=1$, $\hat y = 0.001$). The gradient contribution is $(0.001 - 1)x = -0.999\,x$ — essentially full strength. A confidently-right point ($y=1$, $\hat y = 0.999$) contributes $-0.001\,x$ — essentially nothing. The loss and the model were designed together so that the saturation cancels exactly. That is also the reason the far-away point at $x=10$ in Figure B left the logistic fit unchanged.

beyond syllabus The Hessian works out to $\nabla^2 J = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \sigma_i(1-\sigma_i)\,x^{(i)}x^{(i)\top}$, a non-negative combination of outer products, hence positive semi-definite — so $J$ is convex in $\theta$. No local minima, unlike squared loss. That is the formal justification for the whole §7.1 detour.

7.4 There is no closed form

Setting the gradient to zero gives the score equations

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma\bigl(\theta^\top x^{(i)}\bigr)\,x^{(i)} \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} y^{(i)}x^{(i)} . $$

In linear regression the analogous step gave $X^\top X\theta = X^\top y$ — linear in $\theta$, so you invert a matrix and you are done. Here $\theta$ is trapped inside $\sigma$, i.e. inside an exponential. The equation is transcendental and has no solution in elementary functions. There is no normal equation for logistic regression.

So you iterate. Since $J$ is convex and differentiable, gradient descent is guaranteed to find the global minimum given a small enough step size:

θ ← 0                                   # or small random values
repeat until converged:
    g ← (1/N) Σ_i ( σ(θᵀx⁽ⁱ⁾) − y⁽ⁱ⁾ ) x⁽ⁱ⁾     # full-batch gradient
    θ ← θ − α·g                         # α is the learning rate

and for large $N$ you use stochastic gradient descent instead — replace the sum over all $N$ samples with a sum over one sample (or a minibatch) chosen at random. The gradient formula is unchanged; only the set you average over shrinks. Everything you learned in chapter 9 about learning rates, convergence and minibatches transfers verbatim.

8. Next step: more than two classes

The notes' third promise. Binary logistic regression outputs one number. For $K$ classes we want a whole distribution, $\hat y_1,\dots,\hat y_K$ with $\hat y_k \ge 0$ and $\sum_k \hat y_k = 1$. Give each class its own parameter vector $\theta_k \in \R^M$, compute $K$ scores $\theta_k^\top x$, and normalise them with the softmax:

Definition $$ \hat y_k \;=\; P(Y = k \mid x) \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(\theta_k^\top x\bigr)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\bigl(\theta_j^\top x\bigr)} $$

$\exp$ makes every score positive; dividing by the sum makes them add to $1$. So the output really is a probability distribution over the $K$ classes, by construction.

Proof

Claim: softmax with $K=2$ is the sigmoid.

$$ \hat y_1 = \frac{e^{\theta_1^\top x}}{e^{\theta_1^\top x} + e^{\theta_0^\top x}} . $$

Divide numerator and denominator by $e^{\theta_1^\top x}$ (non-zero, so legal):

$$ \hat y_1 = \frac{1}{1 + e^{\theta_0^\top x - \theta_1^\top x}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\theta_1 - \theta_0)^\top x}} = \sigma\bigl((\theta_1-\theta_0)^\top x\bigr). \qquad\blacksquare $$

So binary logistic regression is the $K=2$ softmax with $\theta = \theta_1 - \theta_0$. Only the difference of the two parameter vectors is identifiable, which is why two classes need one vector, not two — and in general why softmax has $K$ vectors but only $K-1$ degrees of freedom (adding a constant vector to every $\theta_k$ cancels top and bottom and leaves all predictions unchanged).

The loss generalises the same way. With the true class $y \in \{1,\dots,K\}$, multi-class cross-entropy is

$$ \ell(y, \hat y) = -\sum_{k=1}^{K}\1\{y = k\}\log\hat y_k \;=\; -\log \hat y_{\,y} $$

where $\1\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator (1 if true, 0 otherwise). Same sentence as before: minus the log of the probability you gave the true class. The binary formula is the $K=2$ case written out. And the gradient keeps its shape too:

$$ \nabla_{\theta_k} J = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Bigl(\hat y^{(i)}_k - \1\{y^{(i)} = k\}\Bigr)x^{(i)} $$

— prediction minus truth, times the input, exactly as in §7.3, now once per class. beyond syllabus This model is variously called softmax regression, multinomial logistic regression, or (in a neural network) "a linear layer with a softmax output" — it is the last layer of essentially every classifier you will ever train.

9. Common mistakes

Common mistake

1. "Logistic regression is regression, so it predicts a number." No — it is a classification model. It is the model this chapter built to solve a classification problem, its labels are in $\{0,1\}$, and its output is used to choose a class.

So why the name? Because of §6. The model performs a linear regression on the log-odds: $\log\frac{p}{1-p} = \theta^\top x$ is a bona fide linear regression, whose response variable happens to be the logit of a probability rather than an observed number. The name records the mathematics of how the model was originally derived in 19th-century statistics, not the task it is used for. It is a historical label, and it misleads roughly one student per semester into fitting it with squared loss and wondering why nothing works.

Common mistake

2. Always thresholding at $0.5$. Nothing in the mathematics says $0.5$ is the right operating point; it is just the value that makes the decision rule coincide with $\theta^\top x \ge 0$. Two situations break it:

  • Class imbalance. If only $1\%$ of samples are class 1, a well-fitted model may never output $h(x) > 0.5$ at all, and thresholding at $0.5$ gives you a classifier that says "class 0" to everything — $99\%$ accurate and completely useless.
  • Asymmetric loss. Recall the asymmetric loss table from chapter 5: if a false negative costs $C_{\text{FN}}$ and a false positive costs $C_{\text{FP}}$, the expected-cost-minimising rule is to predict class 1 when $h(x)\,C_{\text{FN}} > (1-h(x))\,C_{\text{FP}}$, i.e. when
$$ h(x) \;>\; \frac{C_{\text{FP}}}{C_{\text{FP}} + C_{\text{FN}}} . $$

If missing a tumour is $9\times$ worse than an unnecessary biopsy, the threshold is $1/(1+9) = 0.1$, not $0.5$. This is exactly why we wanted probabilities in §1 — a probability lets you move the threshold after training; a hard label does not.

Common mistake

3. Forgetting the bias term. If you write $h(x) = \sigma(w^\top \tilde x)$ with no $\theta_0$, the decision boundary is $w^\top \tilde x = 0$, a hyperplane forced through the origin. Your model can rotate the boundary but never translate it. On our 1-D dataset that means the boundary is stuck at $x = 0$ instead of $x = 1.05$, and five points are misclassified no matter what $w$ you pick.

In practice the bug looks like this: you build the design matrix from your features, forget to prepend the column of ones (the same column chapter 10 added to $\Phi$), and get an accuracy that is stubbornly mediocre with no error message. Always check that $\theta$ has $M+1$ entries when you have $M$ features.

Common mistake

4. Assuming $\sigma(\theta^\top x)$ is a calibrated probability. The output is a number in $(0,1)$ and we interpret it as $P(Y=1\mid x)$. That interpretation is an assumption about the model class — namely that the true log-odds really are linear in $x$. When that is roughly right (and the training loss really is cross-entropy, which is what pushes the outputs toward the empirical rates), the numbers come out well calibrated. When it is not, they can be systematically off even while the rankings and the accuracy are fine.

How to check. Bin your held-out predictions — say all samples with $h(x) \in [0.7, 0.8)$ — and compute what fraction of them were actually class 1. Calibrated means the fraction is about $0.75$. Plot bin-midpoint against observed frequency (a reliability diagram); calibrated is the diagonal. Regularisation, class rebalancing, and any tree-ensemble or neural model all routinely distort this. beyond syllabus Post-hoc fixes exist (Platt scaling, isotonic regression) but the first step is simply to look.

Common mistake

5. Small numerical traps. (a) Computing $\sigma(z)$ as 1/(1+exp(-z)) overflows for very negative $z$; libraries use the branch $\sigma(z) = e^{z}/(1+e^{z})$ when $z < 0$. (b) $\log(0)$ is $-\infty$, so never round $\hat y$ to $0$ or $1$ before taking the log — this is why real implementations fuse the sigmoid and the log into one "logits" function. (c) The range is $(0,1)$ open, so a printed $\hat y$ of exactly $1.0$ means floating-point rounding, not certainty.

10. Recap

  1. A hard class label tells you nothing about strength of belief, so we want $P(Y = k \mid x)$ instead.
  2. Decision trees classify non-linearly; logistic regression classifies while staying a linear model, keeping interpretable coefficients and easy optimisation.
  3. Plain linear regression on $\{0,1\}$ labels produces outputs outside $[0,1]$ and is dragged around by far-away points.
  4. Thresholding fixes the outputs but makes the model absurdly certain and — fatally — gives a derivative of zero almost everywhere, so gradient descent cannot move.
  5. The logistic function $\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})$ maps $\R$ smoothly onto $(0,1)$, with $\sigma(0) = 0.5$, asymptotes at $0$ and $1$, $\sigma(-z) = 1-\sigma(z)$, and the crucial identity $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$.
  6. The model is $h(x) = \sigma(\theta^\top x) = P(Y=1\mid x)$, with $P(Y=0\mid x) = 1-h(x)$.
  7. $h(x)\ge 0.5 \iff \theta^\top x \ge 0$, a hyperplane — so it is a linear classifier. The direction of $w$ orients the boundary; $\|w\|$ sets the sharpness; $\theta_0$ slides it.
  8. $\log\frac{h}{1-h} = \theta^\top x$: a unit increase in $x_j$ multiplies the odds by $e^{\theta_j}$.
  9. Train with cross-entropy $\ell = -[y\log\hat y + (1-y)\log(1-\hat y)]$, not squared loss (non-convex, vanishing gradients).
  10. $\nabla_\theta J = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i(\sigma(\theta^\top x^{(i)}) - y^{(i)})x^{(i)}$ — same form as linear regression. No closed form, so run GD or SGD.
  11. $K$ classes: softmax $\hat y_k = e^{\theta_k^\top x}/\sum_j e^{\theta_j^\top x}$, which is the sigmoid when $K=2$, with multi-class cross-entropy $-\log\hat y_y$.
Which single property of $\sigma$ is the reason the sigmoid replaced the threshold function?
Why: all four statements are true of $\sigma$, but only the last one is a property the threshold function lacks and that we could not do without. The threshold already outputs valid classes (so A was not the blocker — though it does not output probabilities), it is also antisymmetric about its jump, and it also induces a linear boundary. What it does not have is a usable derivative: $g'_{\text{threshold}} = 0$ almost everywhere, so $\nabla_\theta J = 0$ and $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\cdot 0$ never moves. Differentiability with non-vanishing slope is the property that makes the model trainable, and trainability is what the notes single out when they call the threshold "very problematic to optimize over."
