Part II · Chapter 6

Decision trees

After this page you can take a table of labelled examples, build the decision tree by hand the way the algorithm would, compute its training error, and say exactly why the error-rate splitting criterion has to be thrown away.

The apartment problem

You are moving to Pittsburgh and there are four hundred apartments listed. You cannot tour four hundred apartments. You need a cheap, fast rule that says go see this one or skip it, and you need to be able to explain that rule to your roommate.

So you write one down. Not as a formula — as a sequence of questions, each one narrowing things down:

Figure 1 · The hand-written apartment tree
What to notice: nothing here is symmetric. The No side of "Rent low?" is willing to accept an expensive place only if it is really close to campus (1 mile); the Yes side, having already saved money on rent, is far more forgiving — it will walk 2 miles, or take a bus. The tree encodes that trade-off without ever writing a utility function.

Read it out loud and it is a policy: if the rent is low and the deposit is low, go. If the rent is low, the deposit is high, but you're within 2 miles, go. If the rent is high, you only bother if it's within a mile. That is a decision tree.

Anatomy, and what interpretability actually buys you

Definition

A decision tree is a hypothesis function $\hat y = h(x)$ built from two kinds of node:

  • Internal nodes split on some attribute (also called a feature) $x_j$ of the input. The value of $x_j$ decides which branch you follow. Two branches is the usual case, but a node can split into more.
  • Leaves carry output labels — here $\checkmark$ or $\times$. Reaching a leaf ends the computation and the leaf's label is the prediction $\hat y$.

The professor flags one benefit in capital letters, and it is the right one to flag:

Key idea

Major benefit: human interpretable. You can print a decision tree on one page, hand it to a person with no mathematical training, and they can both execute it and argue with it.

Be honest about what that is worth, because "interpretable" gets used as a magic word. Here is the real ledger.

What interpretability buys

You can audit the model: "it rejected this apartment because the deposit was high and it's 2.3 miles out" is a complete, checkable explanation. You can debug it: a nonsense split is visible instantly. You can deploy it legally in domains (credit, medicine) where a decision must be justified. And you can edit it by hand — bolt on a rule the data never saw.

What it costs

Accuracy, on hard problems. A neural network for image classification has millions of parameters and no human-readable story; a tree that tried to classify raw pixels with "is pixel 4,117 bright?" would be both huge and terrible. Trees also carve feature space into axis-aligned boxes (see below), so a genuinely diagonal rule costs them a staircase of splits.

Analogy

A decision tree is a triage flowchart taped to the wall of an emergency room. A neural network is an experienced attending physician. The attending is usually more accurate. But when the flowchart is wrong you can find the box and fix it, and when the attending is wrong all you can do is ask them to explain, and hope the explanation is the actual reason.

Where the analogy breaks: the flowchart on the wall was written by a committee of experts. The whole point of this chapter is that we are going to learn the flowchart from data instead.

Rule-based vs. learned: the actual ML question

Look again at Figure 1 and ask: where did it come from? You made it up. You already knew that rent matters more than bus access, and you guessed the thresholds. That is a rule-based approach, and it is perfectly legitimate when you have the rules.

The notes' objection is two-part, and both halves matter:

  • You often don't have the rules. Nobody can write down, from first principles, which combination of 40 blood-panel numbers indicates sepsis.
  • The setting is too complex. Even where rules exist, with $d = 30$ binary attributes there are $2^{30}$ possible inputs and astronomically many trees. Hand-authoring is hopeless.
Key idea

The ML question for this chapter: given a set of labelled examples, how do you construct a decision tree?

Everything that follows — stumps, majority vote, the recursion, BestAttribute, entropy in Chapter 7 — is an answer to that one question.

What the examples comprise

Before you can learn anything you have to say precisely what one "example" is. In the apartment problem:

  • Each example is one prospective apartment.
  • $x$ is the vector of answers to the five questions: "Rent low?", "Deposit low?", "Within 1 mi?", "Within 2 mi?", "Near bus stop?"
  • $y$ is the answer to "should I go check this apartment out or not?"

Written formally, exactly as the notes have it:

$$N = \#\text{ prospective apartments},\qquad \X = \{\text{Yes},\text{No}\}^5 = \{0,1\}^5, \qquad \Y = \{Y, N\} = \{0,1\}$$
Common mistake

Read those three lines slowly, because every symbol is doing work.

  • $N$ is a count of examples (rows of your table), not a count of attributes.
  • $\X$ is the input space — the set of all possible $x$, not the set you happen to have. $\{0,1\}^5$ has $2^5 = 32$ elements; your dataset might contain 12 apartments, some repeated.
  • The superscript in $x^{(i)}$ is an index, not a power. $x^{(3)}$ is the third apartment. $x^{(3)}_2$ is the third apartment's answer to question 2. The subscript indexes attributes, the parenthesised superscript indexes examples.
  • Encoding Yes as $1$ and No as $0$ is a choice. Nothing breaks if you flip it; the learned tree just flips its Yes/No branches.

Having fixed the concrete problem, generalise. For the rest of the chapter:

$$\X = \{0,1\}^d \ \ (d \text{ binary attributes}), \qquad \Y = \{0,1\}$$

Inference: running a new point down the tree

Once you have a tree, prediction is trivial and that is the point. Given a new apartment $x^{(\mathrm{new})} \in \X$, start at the root, evaluate the question there, follow the matching branch, repeat, and output the label of whatever leaf you land in.

Example

Take $x^{(\mathrm{new})} = (\text{rent low}=\text{Yes},\ \text{deposit low}=\text{No},\ \text{1mi}=\text{No},\ \text{2mi}=\text{No},\ \text{bus}=\text{Yes})$.

Root "Rent low?" → Yes → "Deposit low?" → No → "Within 2mi of campus?" → No → "Near bus stop?" → Yes → leaf $\checkmark$.

Go see it. Cost: four comparisons. Note that attribute "Within 1mi" was never even read — a tree only looks at the features it needs, which is another quiet advantage over models that must evaluate every coordinate.

A tree over $d = 5$ binary attributes has depth 3 along the path a particular $x$ takes. How many of the 5 attribute values of that $x$ did the tree actually need to look at?
Why: Inference reads one attribute per internal node it passes through, so a depth-3 path reads at most 3 attributes (fewer if the tree reuses an attribute). The model is defined for all of $\{0,1\}^5$, but it consumes only what its path needs. This is why trees are cheap at test time and why they cope gracefully with features that are expensive to measure — an unused feature never has to be collected.

The key building block: the decision stump

Trees are recursive, so we should start with the smallest possible tree that still does something. The notes call it the key building block:

Definition

A decision stump is a decision tree with exactly one split: a single internal node "Is a certain attribute $= 1$?" branching straight into two leaves, each carrying a chosen output label.

Figure 2 · The decision stump, the atom of every tree
What to notice: a stump has exactly two degrees of freedom — the attribute at the node and the two leaf labels. With $d$ binary attributes and binary labels there are only $d \times 2 \times 2 = 4d$ possible stumps, so you can brute-force the best one.

Constructing a stump, in three steps

The pre-reading gives the recipe:

  1. Select the attribute and split criterion. Which feature does the node test, and what test? For binary features the test is just "$x_b = 1$?"; for real-valued features you also pick a threshold.
  2. Split the training dataset into subsets by passing each training point through the decision node. Each leaf then holds a subset of the training data.
  3. Each leaf predicts by majority vote among the output values $y^{(i)}$ of the points that landed there. A tie-breaking scheme must be specified.
Definition

Majority vote. Given a leaf's subset of training points, each with a discrete label $y^{(i)} \in \Y$:

$$\hat y \;=\; \argmax_{k \in \Y} \; \sum_i \1\!\left(y^{(i)} = k\right)$$

In words, symbol by symbol: $\1(\cdot)$ is the indicator function, which is $1$ when the statement inside is true and $0$ otherwise. So $\sum_i \1(y^{(i)} = k)$ simply counts how many points in this leaf have label $k$. The $\argmax_{k}$ then returns the label $k$ with the biggest count. Put plainly: the majority vote is the output class that appears most often in the training subset at that leaf.

Common mistake

$\argmax$ returns the argument (which label), not the value (how many). If a leaf holds labels $\{0,0,1\}$, then $\sum_i \1(y^{(i)}=0) = 2$ and $\sum_i \1(y^{(i)}=1)=1$, so the $\max$ is $2$ but the $\argmax$ — and therefore $\hat y$ — is $\mathbf{0}$.

And notice why a tie-break rule is genuinely needed: with labels $\{0,0,1,1\}$ the $\argmax$ is not unique, so the formula alone does not define $\hat y$. Convention used throughout this guide: on a tie, predict $1$. Any fixed rule works; what is unacceptable is leaving it unstated, because then two people compute different training errors for the same tree.

Why majority vote is the right leaf label

Proof

Claim: among all constant predictors on a leaf, majority vote minimises the number of training mistakes on that leaf.

Let the leaf hold $n_k$ points of label $k$, with $n = \sum_k n_k$ total. A constant predictor $\hat y = c$ is wrong on every point whose label is not $c$, so it makes

$$\text{mistakes}(c) \;=\; n - n_c$$

mistakes. Since $n$ is fixed, minimising $n - n_c$ over $c$ is the same as maximising $n_c$ over $c$, and $\argmax_c n_c$ is exactly the majority vote. $\blacksquare$

This is why leaves are never a design decision: given the tree's structure, the optimal leaf labels are forced. All the intelligence in tree learning lives in choosing the splits.

The recursive construction algorithm

From a stump to a tree is one idea: do it again on each leaf. Each leaf holds a subset of the data; run the stump procedure on that subset; repeat until a stopping criterion fires. Here is the algorithm exactly as the professor's notes state it.

Algorithm 1  Decision Tree Construction

1: function DT(dataset S, set of attributes A ⊆ {1, 2, ..., d})
2:     if A = ∅  or  all examples in S have the same label then
3:         return a leaf with value = majority vote of labels in S
4:     end if
5:     let b = BestAttribute(S, A) ∈ A
6:     let LeftNode  = DT( {(x,y) ∈ S | x_b = 1},  A \ {b} )
7:     let RightNode = DT( {(x,y) ∈ S | x_b = 0},  A \ {b} )
8:     return the tree           "Is the b-th attribute equal to 1?"
                                    /  Yes            No  \
                                LeftNode            RightNode
9: end function

It is called initially with the whole training set and the whole attribute list:

$$S = \{(x^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}, \qquad A = \{1, 2, \dots, d\}$$

The recursion in words, line by line

  • Line 2 — the two base cases. $A = \emptyset$ means you have run out of questions to ask: every attribute has already been used on the path down to here, so no further split is even possible. "All examples in $S$ have the same label" means the node is pure — splitting it cannot reduce training error below the zero it already has.
  • Line 3 — the leaf. Majority vote, as proved above. Note it is needed even in the first base case, where $S$ may well be mixed.
  • Line 5 — the only interesting line. BestAttribute is a black box for now. Swapping in different implementations of it is what distinguishes the algorithms in the literature; that is the subject of the BestAttribute section and all of Chapter 7.
  • Lines 6–7 — the partition. Every point in $S$ goes to exactly one child, because $x_b$ is either $1$ or $0$ and never both. The two children partition $S$: $|S_{\text{left}}| + |S_{\text{right}}| = |S|$. And $b$ is removed from $A$, so the sub-problems are strictly smaller in both arguments — which is why the recursion terminates: depth can be at most $d$.
  • Line 8 — assembling. The recursive calls return whole sub-trees, not labels. You hang them off the new node and return the result upward.
Intuition

This is the same shape as every divide-and-conquer algorithm you already know. Quicksort picks a pivot and recurses on two sub-arrays; DT picks an attribute and recurses on two sub-datasets. The difference is that quicksort's pivot choice only affects speed, whereas DT's attribute choice affects the answer. That is why BestAttribute gets a whole chapter of theory and quicksort's pivot rule gets a footnote.

Figure 3 · The call tree of DT on the 8-row dataset (the recursion, not the model)
What to notice: the call tree and the model tree have the same shape. Every recursive call becomes an internal node; every base case becomes a leaf. Drawing the recursion is drawing the tree.

A wrinkle worth naming: "Is $x_b = 1$?" vs "Is $x_b = 0$?"

The pseudocode always asks "Is $x_b = 1$?" and sends the Yes group left. But the tree the notes present for the worked example asks "Is $x_2 = 0$?". These are not different algorithms — asking "is it 0?" is the same split as asking "is it 1?" with the two branches swapped. Which phrasing you write down is cosmetic. Keep the partition straight and the labels will take care of themselves.

BestAttribute, version 1: minimise training error

We need to fill in BestAttribute. The notes' first proposal is the most obvious one imaginable:

Key idea

Pretend you are allowed only one split — i.e. you must produce a decision stump. Then ask: which choice of attribute to split on (and which labels for the two resulting leaves) gives the lowest training error? Pick that attribute.

This is a finite search you can do by hand. For each candidate attribute $b \in A$: split $S$ into the $x_b=1$ group and the $x_b=0$ group, majority-vote each group, count the mistakes in each group, add them. The attribute with the smallest total wins.

The leaf labels do not need a separate search, because of the proof above: given the split, majority vote is already optimal. So "choose attribute and choose labels" collapses to "choose attribute".

Suppose a stubborn colleague insists the root must test $x_3$. Build the rest of the tree greedily from there (splitting the children on the best remaining attribute), and report the final training error. Is a perfect fit still reachable?

Step 1 — Perform the root split on $x_3$

$x_3 = 1$: rows 2, 4, 6, 8 with labels $0,1,1,1$ (attributes $x_1,x_2$ left).
$x_3 = 0$: rows 1, 3, 5, 7 with labels $0,0,1,1$.

Neither child is pure, so both recurse with $A = \{1,2\}$.

Step 2 — Recurse on the $x_3 = 1$ child (rows 2, 4, 6, 8)

Their $(y, x_1, x_2)$: row 2 = $(0,1,0)$, row 4 = $(1,0,0)$, row 6 = $(1,1,1)$, row 8 = $(1,1,1)$.

Split on $x_1$: $x_1{=}1$ → rows 2,6,8 with labels $0,1,1$ → vote 1, 1 mistake. $x_1{=}0$ → row 4, label 1 → 0 mistakes. Total 1.

Split on $x_2$: $x_2{=}1$ → rows 6,8, labels $1,1$ → 0 mistakes. $x_2{=}0$ → rows 2,4, labels $0,1$ → tie → vote 1, 1 mistake. Total 1.

A tie. Take $x_2$ (lowest index among the winners is a fine deterministic rule; the notes do not specify one, so state yours). Then the $x_2{=}1$ leaf is pure, and the $x_2{=}0$ node still holds rows 2 and 4 with labels $0,1$ and $A = \{1\}$ remaining. Split on $x_1$: row 2 has $x_1{=}1$ (label 0), row 4 has $x_1{=}0$ (label 1). Both children pure! So this whole branch reaches 0 mistakes at depth 3.

Step 3 — Recurse on the $x_3 = 0$ child (rows 1, 3, 5, 7)

Their $(y, x_1, x_2)$: row 1 = $(0,1,0)$, row 3 = $(0,1,0)$, row 5 = $(1,1,1)$, row 7 = $(1,1,1)$.

Split on $x_1$: every row has $x_1 = 1$. The $x_1{=}0$ child is empty and the $x_1{=}1$ child is the whole set — a useless split, 2 mistakes.

Split on $x_2$: $x_2{=}1$ → rows 5,7 (labels $1,1$, pure); $x_2{=}0$ → rows 1,3 (labels $0,0$, pure). 0 mistakes.

So $x_2$ wins here and finishes the branch at depth 2.

Answer & the takeaway

Final training error: 0. The forced-$x_3$ tree still fits perfectly — but it needs 4 internal nodes and depth 3, where the greedy tree needed 2 internal nodes and depth 2.

Takeaway. A bad first split usually does not destroy your ability to fit the training data; with enough attributes you can almost always drive training error to zero eventually. What it destroys is compactness. And compactness is not cosmetic: a bigger tree means fewer examples per leaf, which means the leaf labels are estimated from less data, which means worse generalisation. BestAttribute is fighting for a small tree, not merely a correct one.

Greediness: why this algorithm is not optimal, and why we do it anyway

The notes are careful to flag this, and you should be too:

Key idea

The construction algorithm is a greedy approach towards ERM (empirical risk minimisation, see Chapter 5). Greediness is suboptimal but helps computational efficiency.

Unpack both halves.

Why it is suboptimal. True ERM would say: search over all trees of some size and return the one with lowest training error. The greedy algorithm instead commits to the root split using a one-step lookahead — it judges an attribute by what it does on its own, immediately, and never reconsiders. A split that looks useless alone can be indispensable in combination.

Why we do it anyway. Finding the smallest tree consistent with a dataset is NP-hard. Greedy costs $O(N \cdot d)$ work per node and is the difference between "trains in a second" and "does not train."

The canonical failure: XOR

Example

Let $d = 2$ and $y = x_1 \oplus x_2$ (exclusive-or). The complete dataset is four rows:

$i$$x_1$$x_2$$y$

Baseline: 2 zeros, 2 ones → majority vote is a tie → predict 1 → 2 mistakes out of 4.

Split on $x_1$: $x_1{=}1$ gives rows 3, 4 with labels $1, 0$ — a 1–1 tie, 1 mistake. $x_1{=}0$ gives rows 1, 2 with labels $0,1$ — another tie, 1 mistake. Total $2/4$. The split bought nothing.

Split on $x_2$: by symmetry, also $2/4$. Also nothing.

So the one-step criterion reports that both attributes are worthless, even though the pair of them determines $y$ exactly. (The same is true of mutual information here: $I(Y;x_1) = I(Y;x_2) = 0$. XOR defeats every one-step-lookahead criterion, not just error rate — this is worth knowing, because students often assume entropy rescues XOR. It does not.)

Intuition

Greedy search is a hiker who always walks in the locally steepest downhill direction. XOR is a landscape where the ground is perfectly flat in both compass directions from where you stand, but there is a deep valley diagonally. No single-direction probe detects it.

How the algorithm still recovers

Here is the part people forget. Run the algorithm literally on XOR:

  1. At the root, all attributes tie. BestAttribute breaks the tie somehow — say it returns $x_1$.
  2. Left child ($x_1 = 1$) holds rows 3, 4 with labels $1, 0$. Not pure, and $A = \{2\}$ is not empty, so recurse. The only choice is $x_2$: row 3 has $x_2{=}0$ (label 1), row 4 has $x_2{=}1$ (label 0). Both children pure.
  3. Right child ($x_1=0$) holds rows 1, 2. Split on $x_2$: both children pure.

Final tree: depth 2, zero training error, perfect. The greedy criterion was blind, but the recursion kept going anyway because the stopping rule was "pure or out of attributes", not "gain is zero".

Common mistake

This is exactly why the stopping criterion "stop if the split doesn't reduce training error by much" — one of the options the notes list — is dangerous. On XOR it stops at the root with error $2/4$ and never discovers the perfect depth-2 tree. The cheap fix used in practice is to grow the tree out fully and then prune it back, rather than stopping early, because pruning judges a subtree by what it achieved, not by what its first split promised. beyond syllabus

On the XOR dataset, the greedy criterion assigns both attributes zero benefit. What does the algorithm as written in the pseudocode actually do?
Why: The base case on line 2 is "$A = \emptyset$ or all examples share a label" — neither is true at the XOR root, so the algorithm must split. It breaks the tie by whatever rule BestAttribute uses, recurses, and at depth 2 the remaining attribute separates each child perfectly. The lesson: greedy scoring can be blind at one level and still be rescued by the recursion below it — provided you did not add an early-stopping rule that quits on zero gain.

Evaluating decision trees

The pre-reading asks a deceptively simple question: here are two decision trees for the Iris task — which is better?

Key idea

You cannot tell. Comparing two models by staring at them is not a thing. You need (a) data and (b) a performance measure.

The measure for classification is the classification error rate:

$$\text{err} \;=\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_i \1\!\left(y^{(i)} \ne \hat y^{(i)}\right)$$

Naming every symbol: $N$ is the number of examples you are evaluating on; $y^{(i)}$ is the true (measured) label of example $i$; $\hat y^{(i)} = h(x^{(i)})$ is what the tree predicts for it; $\1(\cdot)$ is again the indicator, contributing $1$ for a mismatch and $0$ for a match. The sum counts mistakes, and dividing by $N$ turns a count into a rate so datasets of different sizes are comparable.

This is the empirical risk $\hat R(h)$ from Chapter 5 with the zero-one loss $\ell(y,\hat y) = \1(y \ne \hat y)$ plugged in. Nothing new — just the general machinery specialised to classification.

Common mistake

Error rate treats all mistakes as equally bad. That is often wrong. The pre-reading's own medical example makes the point with an asymmetric loss table, where predicting "healthy" for an ill patient (a false negative) costs $10$ and the reverse costs $2$:

$\ell(y, \hat y)$$\hat y = 0$$\hat y = 1$
$y = 0$02
$y = 1$100

Everything in this chapter works with a weighted loss too — you just replace "count the mistakes" with "add up the losses" in the majority vote and in BestAttribute. The leaf then predicts the cost-minimising class rather than the most common one.

Extension: beyond binary attributes, and decision boundaries

The notes list several extensions. Start with the one that changes the picture most.

Attributes with more than $\{0,1\}$ values

Two cases, and they are handled differently.

  • Categorical with $m$ values (e.g. colour $\in$ {red, green, blue}). Either split the node into $m$ branches at once, or binarise: test "colour = red?" and let the No branch test the rest. Multi-way splits consume the attribute entirely in one node; binary tests let you come back to it.
  • Real-valued (e.g. petal length $\in \R$). You cannot enumerate the values, so the node tests a threshold: "Is $x_j \ge t$?" Now BestAttribute must choose both $j$ and $t$.

Here is the notes' example with $\X = \R^2$, $\Y = \{0,1\}$, splitting first at $x_2 = 4.5$ and then at $x_1 = 3.5$:

Figure 9 · A real-valued tree and the regions it carves in $\R^2$
What to notice: the root's test $x_2 \ge 4.5$ draws a line across the whole plane. The second test $x_1 \le 3.5$ draws a line only inside the lower half — because that node is only reached when $x_2 < 4.5$. The depth of a node is exactly how many constraints its region satisfies.
Definition

The decision boundary is the set of lines (in general, surfaces) that separate the region where the model outputs $0$ from the region where it outputs $1$.

In Figure 9 it is the horizontal segment at $x_2 = 4.5$ over $x_1 \le 3.5$, plus the vertical segment at $x_1 = 3.5$ below $x_2 = 4.5$. Notice it is not the whole of either line: where label-1 sits on both sides of a line, that stretch of line is not a boundary at all.

Why tree boundaries are always axis-aligned staircases

Proof

Every internal node tests a single coordinate against a constant: $x_j \ge t$. The set $\{x : x_j \ge t\}$ is a half-space whose bounding hyperplane is perpendicular to axis $j$ — i.e. axis-aligned.

A leaf is reached exactly when all the tests on its root-to-leaf path hold, so the region a leaf owns is an intersection of axis-aligned half-spaces, which is an axis-aligned box (possibly unbounded).

The model's $0$-region is a union of such boxes, and so is its $1$-region. A boundary between two unions of axis-aligned boxes can only consist of axis-aligned pieces. $\blacksquare$

Consequence for a diagonal boundary. Suppose the truth is $y = \1(x_1 + x_2 \ge 5)$ — a clean 45° line. A tree can never draw that line. It can only approximate it with a staircase: many alternating horizontal and vertical splits, each shaving off one small rectangle. To get within $\varepsilon$ you need roughly $O(1/\varepsilon)$ splits, and every extra split subdivides the data further, so the leaves get sparse and the fit gets noisy.

Intuition

A decision tree can only cut with a guillotine held parallel to the table edges. It can approximate a diagonal cut, but only by making a very large number of tiny straight cuts — and each cut costs it data.

The fix is not a better tree algorithm, it is a better feature: add the column $z = x_1 + x_2$ and one split on $z$ does the job. That is precisely the subject of feature engineering.

How threshold candidates are chosen beyond syllabus

Every student asks this, so: for a real-valued attribute $x_j$, you do not search all of $\R$. Sort the $N$ training values of $x_j$, and consider only the midpoints between consecutive distinct sorted values as candidate thresholds.

The reason is that the partition of the training data only changes when $t$ crosses a data value. Between two consecutive values, every $t$ induces exactly the same split and therefore exactly the same training error. There are at most $N-1$ distinct partitions, so at most $N-1$ candidates. Putting $t$ at the midpoint rather than on a data point is a small extra kindness: it maximises the margin to the nearest training points, which usually generalises a touch better.

Example

Petal lengths $1.4,\ 1.5,\ 4.1,\ 4.5,\ 4.8,\ 5.1,\ 6.0$. Candidate thresholds: $1.45,\ 2.8,\ 4.3,\ 4.65,\ 4.95,\ 5.55$. Six candidates for this attribute, not infinitely many. The tree in Figure 8 used $2.5$, which is inside the wide gap $(1.5, 4.1)$ and induces the same split as the candidate $2.8$.

Extensions: attribute reuse and stopping criteria

You need not remove the chosen attribute

Line 6–7 of the pseudocode pass $A \setminus \{b\}$ down. The notes point out this is optional: need not remove the chosen attribute when calling the function again in the recursion.

Why it is harmless to keep $b$: for binary attributes, re-splitting on $b$ in a child is merely wasteful, never wrong. Every point in the left child already has $x_b = 1$, so a second test on $b$ sends all of them one way and leaves the other child empty. It gains nothing, but it breaks nothing.

Common mistake

For real-valued attributes you must not remove the attribute. Look at Figure 9 and imagine the truth were a horizontal band $2 \le x_2 \le 4.5$. You need two thresholds on $x_2$ — one at $4.5$, another at $2$ further down the tree. Delete $x_2$ from $A$ after the first split and that band becomes unlearnable.

Real implementations (CART, scikit-learn) therefore never remove attributes. The consequence is that the depth bound "at most $d$" evaporates, and you need a real stopping criterion instead.

Key idea

Stopping criteria exist because of overfitting. Grown to purity, a tree memorises the training set: it will happily build a leaf holding a single noisy example and predict that example's label for the whole region around it. Training error $\to 0$; test error climbs.

Every criterion above is a way of saying "stop trusting the data before the leaves get too small to trust." This is the same bias–variance tension you meet again with regularisation in feature engineering and overfitting.

Intuition

Depth limit and minimum leaf size are two ways to enforce one rule: never make a claim about a region of feature space based on two data points. A leaf holding 40 examples that are 90% positive is a claim you can defend. A leaf holding 1 example is an anecdote.

Extension: the menu of BestAttribute criteria

The notes give three options. Here they are, with the formula for each.

1. Minimise training error

Done above, in full. Split $S$ on each candidate attribute, majority-vote both children, add the mistakes, take the minimum. Simple, intuitive — and, as the next section shows, badly behaved.

2. Minimise Gini impurity beyond syllabus

The notes say "won't discuss here", so one line: the Gini impurity of a node with class proportions $p_k$ is

$$G \;=\; 1 - \sum_{k \in \Y} p_k^2,$$

and you choose the split minimising the weighted average of $G$ over the children — exactly the same weighting scheme as conditional entropy. It is the probability that two points drawn at random from the node have different labels. It is the default in CART and scikit-learn, behaves almost identically to entropy, and is cheaper because it needs no logarithm.

3. Maximise mutual information

Instead of asking "how many mistakes would a stump make?", ask "how much of the uncertainty in the labels does this attribute remove?" Quantifying that requires entropy, which is the whole of Chapter 7. The criterion, stated now so you know where you are going:

$$b \;=\; \argmax_{a \in A} \; I(Y; a) \;=\; \argmax_{a \in A} \big[\,H(Y) - H(Y \mid a)\,\big]$$

This is also called the information gain method.

Why error rate is a broken splitting criterion

The pre-reading drops a warning and moves on: "Later we'll learn that error rate as a splitting criterion is problematic and will replace it with other splitting criteria such as mutual information." That sentence is the hinge between this chapter and the next, so it deserves a concrete demonstration rather than a promise.

Key idea

The defect: error rate only counts which class wins in each child. It is completely blind to by how much. A child that goes from 50/50 to 75/25 has become dramatically more certain, but the majority label did not change, so the error count did not change either. Error rate cannot see progress that does not immediately flip a prediction.

Common mistake

All three numbers are 2/8. Under the error-rate criterion, splitting on $a$, splitting on $b$, and not splitting at all are indistinguishable. The criterion has no opinion.

But the splits are obviously not equally good. Split $b$ finishes half the dataset permanently — the $b=1$ child is pure and will never need another node. Split $a$ leaves both children in exactly the same 3-to-1 state the parent was in; it achieved literally nothing.

$$H(Y) = -\tfrac{6}{8}\log_2\tfrac{6}{8} - \tfrac{2}{8}\log_2\tfrac{2}{8} = 0.75(0.41504) + 0.25(2) = 0.31128 + 0.5 = 0.81128$$ $$H(Y \mid a) = \tfrac{4}{8}(0.81128) + \tfrac{4}{8}(0.81128) = 0.81128 \;\Longrightarrow\; I(Y;a) = 0.81128 - 0.81128 = \mathbf{0}$$ $$H(Y \mid b) = \tfrac{4}{8}(0) + \tfrac{4}{8}(1) = 0.5 \;\Longrightarrow\; I(Y;b) = 0.81128 - 0.5 = \mathbf{0.31128}$$
Key idea

Mutual information says, correctly and unambiguously: $a$ is worthless ($I = 0$, the children are statistically identical to the parent) and $b$ is valuable ($I = 0.311$ bits, it purified half the data). Error rate could not tell them apart.

That is the reason the course switches criteria. Error rate is a fine way to evaluate a finished tree — it is the thing you actually care about. It is a bad way to choose a split, because choosing a split is about making progress toward a future prediction, and progress is measured in certainty, not in flipped labels.

Guided problemGrow the broken-criterion dataset to completion

Using the 8-row $(y, a, b)$ dataset above, build the full tree twice: once with a BestAttribute that breaks ties by preferring $a$, and once preferring $b$. Compare final trees. Assume attributes are removed from $A$ after use, and tie-break majority votes to $1$.

Step 1 — The "prefer $a$" tree

Root splits on $a$. Children: $a{=}1$ holds rows 1,2,5,7 (labels $1,1,1,0$); $a{=}0$ holds rows 3,4,6,8 (labels $1,1,1,0$). Neither pure; $A = \{b\}$ remains.

$a{=}1$ child, split on $b$: $b{=}1$ → rows 1,2 (labels $1,1$, pure); $b{=}0$ → rows 5,7 (labels $1,0$, tie → 1 mistake). $A$ is now empty, so both become leaves.

$a{=}0$ child, split on $b$: $b{=}1$ → rows 3,4 (pure, label 1); $b{=}0$ → rows 6,8 (labels $1,0$, tie → 1 mistake).

Result: 3 internal nodes, 4 leaves, depth 2, training error $2/8$.

Step 2 — The "prefer $b$" tree

Root splits on $b$. $b{=}1$ → rows 1,2,3,4, all label 1 → pure, immediate leaf. $b{=}0$ → rows 5,6,7,8 with labels $1,1,0,0$; $A = \{a\}$.

Split that child on $a$: $a{=}1$ → rows 5,7 (labels $1,0$, tie, 1 mistake); $a{=}0$ → rows 6,8 (labels $1,0$, tie, 1 mistake). $A$ empty, both leaves.

Result: 2 internal nodes, 3 leaves, depth 2, training error $2/8$.

Step 3 — Why is the training error the same?

Because with only two attributes there are four possible $(a,b)$ cells, and the data forces it. Look at the cells: $(1,1)$ holds rows 1,2 — both label 1. $(0,1)$ holds rows 3,4 — both label 1. $(1,0)$ holds rows 5,7 — labels $1,0$. $(0,0)$ holds rows 6,8 — labels $1,0$.

The two mixed cells each contain one point of each label, so no function of $(a,b)$ can classify all eight rows: the data is not separable by these features. Two mistakes is the floor. Both trees reach the floor.

Answer & the takeaway

Both trees hit the irreducible error of $2/8$, but the "prefer $b$" tree does it with one fewer internal node and one fewer leaf, because $b$ cleared half the dataset in a single move.

The takeaway, and the reason for Chapter 7: the criterion that could have told you to prefer $b$ is mutual information ($0.311$ vs $0.000$), not error rate (a dead tie). When two models have equal training error, the simpler one is preferable — fewer leaves means more data per leaf means a more trustworthy prediction. Error rate is structurally unable to express that preference; entropy is.

A node holds 100 points: 60 label-1 and 40 label-0. A candidate split produces children of (50 pos, 10 neg) and (10 pos, 30 neg). What does the error-rate criterion say about this split, and is it right?
Why: Parent error = 40 (minority count). Children: first votes 1 with 10 mistakes, second votes 0 with 10 mistakes, total 20. So error rate does register this split. The point of the comparison is different: error rate only ever counts minority points, so many structurally different splits map to the same score, and it will happily call a split that produces a pure child equal to one that does not. Entropy separates them because it charges for how mixed each child is, not just how many points are on the losing side.

Recap

  • A decision tree is internal nodes that split on attributes plus leaves that carry labels. Its headline benefit is human interpretability; its cost is accuracy on hard, non-axis-aligned problems.
  • Hand-written trees are rule-based. The ML question is how to construct a tree from $N$ labelled examples with $\X = \{0,1\}^d$, $\Y = \{0,1\}$.
  • Inference is just walking root-to-leaf. Training is the recursive algorithm DT(S, A): stop if $A = \emptyset$ or $S$ is pure, otherwise pick $b = $ BestAttribute, partition on $x_b$, recurse.
  • Leaves predict by majority vote $\hat y = \argmax_k \sum_i \1(y^{(i)}=k)$, which provably minimises mistakes at a leaf, with a stated tie-break.
  • BestAttribute v1 = minimise the training error of the resulting stump. On the poll dataset this picks $x_2$ ($1/8$) over $x_1$ and $x_3$ (both $3/8$), and one further split on $x_1$ reaches zero training error.
  • The algorithm is greedy — a one-step lookahead approximation to ERM. XOR defeats the lookahead, but the recursion still recovers the perfect tree, as long as you did not add a zero-gain early stop.
  • Evaluation uses classification error rate $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \1(y^{(i)} \ne \hat y^{(i)})$: Model A $1/7$ beats Model B $2/7$ on the Iris data.
  • Extensions: multi-valued and real-valued attributes (threshold tests at sorted midpoints), axis-aligned staircase decision boundaries, keeping attributes in $A$, and the whole menu of stopping criteria, which exist to control overfitting.
  • Error rate is a fine evaluation measure and a bad splitting criterion, because it is blind to purity changes that do not flip a majority. Mutual information is not. Next chapter.
